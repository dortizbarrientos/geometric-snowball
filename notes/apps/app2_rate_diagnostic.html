APP 02 / 05 · LOGIT IDENTITY & RATE DIAGNOSTIC

The Rate Diagnostic
$r(t)$

"Can we detect that a predictive regime is eroding before the accuracy actually drops?"

Prerequisite: App 1 Key identity: $\dot{\mathcal{A}}_g = \mathcal{A}_g(1-\mathcal{A}_g)\,r(t)$ Notebook: Section 4
01

The diagnostic question

App 1 defined $\mathcal{A}_g$ as a snapshot — the fraction of heritable fitness variance captured by the linear predictor at this moment. But a snapshot is not a warning. A population can sit well inside a predictive channel ($\mathcal{A}_g = 0.85$) while already moving toward its boundary.

What we need is a rate: is $\mathcal{A}_g$ rising or falling, and how fast? That rate is $r(t)$, and it acts like a speedometer pointed at the channel boundary.

Think of driving toward a cliff in fog. The cliff is the channel boundary ($\mathcal{A}_g = \alpha$). Your position ($\mathcal{A}_g$) tells you how close you are. Your speed and direction ($r(t)$) tells you whether you are approaching or retreating — and whether to brake now, before the fog clears.

$r > 0$ means the channel is deepening. $r < 0$ means it is eroding. $r = 0$ means the regime is instantaneously stable. The sign flips before $\mathcal{A}_g$ reaches the threshold.

$\mathcal{A}_g$ lives in $[0,1]$. Its rate of change $\dot{\mathcal{A}}_g$ is bounded and depends on both the current value and the rate of change of the underlying variance components. To decouple the rate from the current position, we apply a logit transform — mapping $[0,1]$ to $(-\infty,+\infty)$ — and differentiate in that unbounded space. The derivative in logit space, $r(t)$, is the clean diagnostic.

In a breeding programme, $\mathcal{A}_g$ may remain above 0.70 for several cycles while $r(t)$ is already negative. Genomic prediction still works today — but the programme is already drifting toward a yield plateau. Measuring $r(t)$ gives a one-generation warning: the genetic architecture is shifting unfavourably before any drop in accuracy is visible in the raw prediction statistics.

02

The logit transform

Define the logit of $\mathcal{A}_g$ as the log-ratio of the variance components:

LOGIT TRANSFORM · Paper Eq. 4
$$\mathcal{L}(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{V_{\rm lin}(t)}{V_{\rm quad}(t)}\right)$$
Note: $\mathcal{L} = \text{logit}(\mathcal{A}_g) = \ln\mathcal{A}_g - \ln(1-\mathcal{A}_g)$, but the log-ratio form is more natural here because it decomposes additively into contributions from each variance component.

The logit transform "uncompresses" the bounded $[0,1]$ scale into an unbounded $(-\infty,+\infty)$ scale. Near the boundaries (very high or very low $\mathcal{A}_g$), small absolute changes in $\mathcal{A}_g$ become large changes in $\mathcal{L}$. This makes small movements near the threshold visible that would otherwise be invisible in the raw $\mathcal{A}_g$ scale.

$\mathcal{L} > 0$ means $V_{\rm lin} > V_{\rm quad}$: the population is inside an additive channel. $\mathcal{L} < 0$ means curvature variance dominates. $\mathcal{L} = 0$ is the boundary, corresponding to $\mathcal{A}_g = 0.5$. The threshold $\alpha = 0.70$ corresponds to $\mathcal{L}_\alpha = \ln(0.70/0.30) \approx 0.847$.

The logit form makes differentiation clean. Using the chain rule:

$$\dot{\mathcal{L}} = \frac{d}{dt}\ln V_{\rm lin} - \frac{d}{dt}\ln V_{\rm quad} = \frac{\dot{V}_{\rm lin}}{V_{\rm lin}} - \frac{\dot{V}_{\rm quad}}{V_{\rm quad}}$$

This is the relative rate of change of each variance component — how fast gradient variance is growing relative to curvature variance. It equals $r(t)$ by definition.

03

The exact identity

The rate diagnostic $r(t) = \dot{\mathcal{L}}$ is linked to $\dot{\mathcal{A}}_g$ by an exact algebraic identity — one that holds for any differentiable $V_{\rm lin}(t)$ and $V_{\rm quad}(t)$, with no approximation.

LOGIT IDENTITY · Paper SI Appendix S2
$$\dot{\mathcal{A}}_g = \mathcal{A}_g\,(1 - \mathcal{A}_g)\,r(t)$$
This is the logistic equation. The factor $\mathcal{A}_g(1-\mathcal{A}_g)$ is maximal at $\mathcal{A}_g = 0.5$ and vanishes at $0$ and $1$. Near the boundary $\alpha = 0.70$, it equals approximately $0.21$, so $r(t)$ must be appreciably nonzero to move $\mathcal{A}_g$.

The identity says: the speed at which the additivity index changes is proportional to (1) how far from the extremes $\mathcal{A}_g$ currently sits, and (2) the underlying rate $r(t)$. When $\mathcal{A}_g$ is very high or very low, even a large $r$ produces little movement — the system is "sticky" at the extremes. The most sensitive region is $\mathcal{A}_g \approx 0.5$.

The equation $\dot{\mathcal{A}}_g = \mathcal{A}_g(1-\mathcal{A}_g)\,r$ is precisely the logistic ODE with a time-varying rate. Its solution has the logistic (S-curve) form: slow near the extremes, fastest near the midpoint. When $r(t)$ is constant, $\mathcal{A}_g(t) = \sigma(\mathcal{L}_0 + rt)$ where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function.

This identity means that $r(t)$ is a leading indicator. A population with $\mathcal{A}_g = 0.85$ and $r = -0.3$ will reach $\mathcal{A}_g = 0.70$ in roughly $(\mathcal{L}_{0.85} - \mathcal{L}_{0.70}) / 0.3 \approx 5$ generations. Measuring $r(t)$ now gives a quantitative forecast of how many generations remain before the channel boundary is crossed.

Proof by direct differentiation. Let $A = V_{\rm lin}/(V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad})$. Then:

$$\dot{A} = \frac{\dot{V}_{\rm lin}(V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad}) - V_{\rm lin}(\dot{V}_{\rm lin}+\dot{V}_{\rm quad})}{(V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad})^2} = \frac{V_{\rm lin}\,V_{\rm quad}}{(V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad})^2}\left(\frac{\dot{V}_{\rm lin}}{V_{\rm lin}} - \frac{\dot{V}_{\rm quad}}{V_{\rm quad}}\right) = A(1-A)\,r$$

The last step uses $V_{\rm lin}\,V_{\rm quad}/(V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad})^2 = A(1-A)$ and $r = \dot{V}_{\rm lin}/V_{\rm lin} - \dot{V}_{\rm quad}/V_{\rm quad}$. No approximations. ∎

🔑

$r(t)$ is observable from changes in variance components alone — it does not require knowing $\dot{\mathcal{A}}_g$ directly.

In a breeding programme or experimental evolution system, $V_{\rm lin}$ and $V_{\rm quad}$ can be estimated from phenotypic and marker data each generation. Their relative rates of change give $r(t)$ without needing to observe the fitness surface directly.
04

Three routes to $r(t)$

Because $V_{\rm lin}$ and $V_{\rm quad}$ both depend on $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{H}$, and $\mathbf{G}$ — each of which can change independently — $r(t)$ decomposes into three mechanistic contributions.

THREE-WAY DECOMPOSITION · Paper Results §3
$$r(t) = \underbrace{r_{\rm motion}}_{\text{mean moves across landscape}} + \underbrace{r_{\rm env}}_{\text{environment reshapes landscape}} + \underbrace{r_{\rm cov}}_{\text{covariance structure changes}}$$
The signs of $r_{\rm env}$ and $r_{\rm cov}$ distinguish failure mode Act II (gradient collapse, $r_{\rm cov} < 0$) from Act III (gradient rotation, $r_{\rm env} \ll 0$).

Motion route ($r_{\rm motion}$): as the population mean moves across a curved landscape, it slides into regions with a different gradient-to-curvature ratio. Gradient collapse near an optimum produces $r_{\rm motion} < 0$.

Environmental route ($r_{\rm env}$): climate change, habitat shifts, or seasonal forcing can rotate or reshape the fitness landscape at the current location. This erodes $\mathcal{A}_g$ without any change in genetic variance.

Covariance route ($r_{\rm cov}$): selection, recombination, mutation, introgression, and drift all reshape $\mathbf{G}$. Each contributes a signed term — directional selection typically makes $r_{\rm cov} > 0$ by compressing variance into gradient-aligned directions.

The decomposition gives a mechanistic diagnosis of prediction failure:

Act II (optimum approach): $r_{\rm env} \approx 0$, $r_{\rm cov} < 0$. The covariance cloud has compressed along the now-irrelevant directional axis. Intervention: introduce new selective targets or expand effective population size.

Act III (gradient rotation): $r_{\rm env} \ll 0$, $r_{\rm cov} \geq 0$. The landscape has rotated but the genetic variance has not. Intervention: introduce variance aligned with the new gradient direction through recombination or introgression.

The same drop in $\mathcal{A}_g$ can come from opposite biological causes. Only $r(t)$'s decomposition distinguishes them.

From the transport laws $\dot{\mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{b}_u\dot{u}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{G}} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{G} - \lambda_{\rm rec}(\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{G}_*) + \mathbf{M} + \mathbf{J} - \mathbf{G}/(2N_e)$, differentiating $V_{\rm lin} = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b}$ gives: $$\dot{V}_{\rm lin} = 2\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\dot{\mathbf{b}} + \mathbf{b}^\top\dot{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{b}$$ The first term generates $r_{\rm motion}$ and $r_{\rm env}$ (via $\dot{\mathbf{b}}$); the second generates $r_{\rm cov}$ (via $\dot{\mathbf{G}}$). An analogous computation for $\dot{V}_{\rm quad}$ produces the curvature contributions. The full derivation is in SI Appendix Section S5.

Section 4 verifies the exact identity $\dot{\mathcal{A}}_g = \mathcal{A}_g(1-\mathcal{A}_g)\,r(t)$ symbolically. The key cell defines AgT and LlogitT as functions of t, computes rT = D[LlogitT, t], and checks FullSimplify[D[AgT,t] - AgT(1-AgT)rT] — which should return exactly 0.

This is a pure symbolic proof: no assumptions, no numerical approximation. Mathematica confirms the identity holds for any differentiable VlinT[t] and VquadT[t].
