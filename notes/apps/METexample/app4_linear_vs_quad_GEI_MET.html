The identification theorem

After App 9, we have $\hat{c}_{11}$ — the total GEI variance. It lumps two biologically distinct things together: the variance of reaction norm slopes ($\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}$) and the variance of reaction norm curvatures ($\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$). These are not interchangeable. Slope variance is linear GEI — predictable from genomic data across environments. Curvature variance is nonlinear GEI — the part that breaks genomic prediction when environments shift beyond the training range.

The split uses only the variance components $\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}$ and $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ from the quadratic reaction norm model already fitted in App 8. The OLS regression from App 9 gives $\hat{c}_{11}$ independently. Their equality — $\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma} = \hat{c}_{11}$ — is the internal consistency check that validates the entire identification.

The biological stakes of the split are high. $V_{\text{lin,GEI}}$ and $V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$ represent two fundamentally different mechanisms of G×E:

  • $V_{\text{lin,GEI}} = \hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} = b_u^\top G b_u$ measures genetic diversity in how strongly genotypes respond to the environmental gradient. High values mean some lines are highly plastic (steep positive or negative slopes) while others are stable. This component is driven by additive alleles that regulate plastic responses — the same alleles that genomic prediction captures well, because their effects are consistent across the environmental range.
  • $V_{\text{quad,GEI}} = \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$ measures genetic diversity in how the curvature of the response varies. High values mean some genotypes are optimal at intermediate environments while others peak at extremes — a genuine niche specialisation. This component is driven by alleles that create non-monotonic fitness responses, and these are precisely the alleles that break genomic prediction when the target environment falls outside the training range.
The breeder's decision rule

When $V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$ is negligible relative to $V_{\text{lin,GEI}}$, Finlay-Wilkinson models suffice and multi-environment genomic prediction is reliable. When $V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$ is substantial, the breeder faces a choice: either restrict predictions to environments within the training range, or explicitly model the curvature using the quadratic reaction norm and accept wider uncertainty in novel environments.

The theorem states that under Gaussian closure and environmental linearity (as derived in the companion paper, §5), the identification is exact:

Identification theorem (companion paper §5, eqs. 11–12)
$$\phi_{\beta\beta} = \operatorname{Var}_z[\beta_i] = \operatorname{Var}_z[b_u^\top \Delta z_i] = b_u^\top G b_u = V_{\text{lin,GEI}}$$ $$\phi_{\gamma\gamma} = \operatorname{Var}_z[\gamma_i] = \operatorname{Var}_z\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta z_i^\top H_u \Delta z_i\right] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2] = V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$$

The second line uses the Isserlis/Wick theorem (established in Paper 1, Section S1.3):

Key lemma — Isserlis theorem applied to quadratic forms
$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta z^\top A \Delta z\right] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(AG)^2], \quad \Delta z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, G)$$

Setting $A = H_u$ gives $\phi_{\gamma\gamma} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$. The internal consistency relation then follows immediately from the structural model:

Internal consistency check
$$c_{11} = \phi_{\beta\beta} + \phi_{\gamma\gamma} = V_{\text{lin,GEI}} + V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$$
Why parity is the key

The cleanness of the split — no cross term between $\phi_{\beta\beta}$ and $\phi_{\gamma\gamma}$ — follows from the same Gaussian parity argument that separates $V_\text{lin}$ from $V_\text{quad}$ in Paper 1. The function $\beta_i = b_u^\top \Delta z_i$ is linear (odd) in $\Delta z_i$, while $\gamma_i = \frac{1}{2}\Delta z_i^\top H_u \Delta z_i$ is quadratic (even). Their product has zero expectation under any symmetric distribution. Parity kills the cross-term, leaving an exact additive decomposition.

2

The GEI additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$

Just as the additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g$ measures the fraction of heritable fitness variance at the reference environment that is captured by the linear (gradient) approximation, the GEI additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$ measures the fraction of total GEI variance that is linear — and therefore predictable from reaction norm slopes alone.

It is computed directly from the $\hat{\Phi}$ matrix of App 8:

GEI additivity index
$$\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})} = \frac{\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}}{\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}}$$

The interpretation is direct. When $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})} > 0.70$ (a reasonable default threshold), Finlay-Wilkinson's linear regression model captures most of the G×E signal, and genomic predictions trained in one environment transfer well to others along the same gradient. When it falls below 0.50, the nonlinear curvature term is doing most of the work, and transferability breaks down.

This number is not the same as the primary additivity index $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g$ from App 7 — they answer different questions. $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g$ asks: how much of the total heritable fitness variance at the reference environment is gradient-aligned? $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$ asks: of the variance that changes with the environment, how much is predictable from linear responsiveness?

Three contrasting scenarios illustrate what $\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$ reveals that standard heritability and G×E statistics cannot:

  • High $\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$, high $c_{11}/c_{00}$ (large linear GEI): Strong, predictable G×E. Genotypes differ markedly in responsiveness, but the ranking by response slope is stable. Multi-environment GBLUP with reaction norm slopes works well. This is the scenario in which mega-environment analysis succeeds — the environmental gradient cleanly separates responsive from stable genotypes, and genomic models trained in one environment predict well in others along the gradient.
  • Low $\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$, high $c_{11}/c_{00}$ (large nonlinear GEI): Strong, unpredictable G×E. Genotype rankings reverse non-monotonically across environments. No amount of extra genomic markers helps if the model is linear. The fitness surface is genuinely curved along the environmental axis. This scenario calls for explicit modelling of $\gamma_i$ and environment-specific predictions rather than cross-environment transfer.
  • Low $\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$, low $c_{11}/c_{00}$ (small total GEI): G×E is negligible. The same genomic model works everywhere. This is the ideal breeding scenario but rare in practice — most crops show substantial G×E when environments span realistic drought/wet or temperature gradients.

$\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$ is a proportion with Var components estimated by REML. Its sampling distribution follows a ratio-of-variance-components distribution. A delta-method approximation for the standard error is:

Delta-method standard error of $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$
$$\operatorname{SE}\!\left[\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}\right] \approx \hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}\left(1 - \hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}\right) \sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta})}{\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}^2} + \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma})}{\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}^2}}$$

The standard errors $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta})$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma})$ are available from the REML output of the App 8 mixed model (the diagonal of the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix of variance parameters). In practice, parametric bootstrapping — simulating datasets from the fitted model and re-estimating $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$ — gives more reliable intervals than the delta method, especially when $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ is small.

Connection to the logit scale

On the logit scale, $\mathcal{L}^{(\text{GEI})} = \ln(\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}/\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma})$, standard errors are more symmetric and confidence intervals are automatically bounded. This mirrors the use of the logit in the main channel diagnostic $\mathcal{L}(t) = \ln(V_\text{lin}/V_\text{quad})$ from Paper 2. App 11 tracks both $\mathcal{L}$ and $\mathcal{L}^{(\text{GEI})}$ in the full decision framework.

The internal consistency check — closing the loop

The framework produces two independent estimates of the total GEI variance: one from the OLS regression of the cross-environment covariance matrix ($\hat{c}_{11}$ from App 9), and one from the reaction norm variance components ($\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ from App 8). Under the model, these must agree.

The discrepancy $\hat{c}_{11} - (\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma})$ is an internal diagnostic under the model assumptions. A small discrepancy (less than 10% of $\hat{c}_{11}$) validates the quadratic reaction norm model and the environmental linearity assumption. A large discrepancy suggests one of:

  • The environmental covariate $u_j$ captures only part of the G×E structure — other environmental axes are important.
  • The Legendre polynomial back-transformation (flagged in App 8) was applied incorrectly.
  • Higher-order GEI (cubic or higher) is present and the quadratic model is insufficient.
  • Sampling noise in one of the two estimators is large relative to the true $c_{11}$.

The consistency check is a model diagnostic for the environmental linearity assumption — the claim that the fitness surface varies linearly with $u$ over the MET range. In biological terms, this assumption says that the direction and magnitude of selection pressure change approximately linearly as the drought index, temperature, or other gradient variable shifts.

This is a reasonable approximation when:

  • The environmental range is moderate (roughly within ±2 standard deviations of the mean environment).
  • There are no major threshold effects — physiological optima, chilling damage thresholds, photoperiod switches — within the trial range.
  • The crop has not been selected exclusively under a narrow set of conditions that made the reference environment unrepresentative of the target population of environments.

When the check fails, the remedy is to add a second environmental covariate ($u_2$, e.g. temperature in addition to water availability), which extends the structural model to a two-gradient version. That extension increases the number of structural coefficients from 3 to 6 and requires $E \geq 6$ environments for identification.

Simulate both estimation routes simultaneously and watch whether they agree. Noise in the covariance matrix (App 9 route) and noise in the variance components (App 8 route) create independent estimation errors. Increase $n$ and $E$ to reduce both.

Let $\hat{c}_{11}^{(\text{OLS})}$ denote the App 9 estimate and $\hat{c}_{11}^{(\text{RN})} = \hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ the App 8 estimate. The discrepancy statistic is:

Consistency discrepancy statistic
$$\delta = \hat{c}_{11}^{(\text{OLS})} - \hat{c}_{11}^{(\text{RN})}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\delta)^2 \approx \operatorname{Var}(\hat{c}_{11}^{(\text{OLS})}) + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}) + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma})$$

A standardised discrepancy $|\delta| / \operatorname{SE}(\delta) > 2$ warrants investigation. This is a model specification test — it checks whether the quadratic reaction norm model is a consistent parametrisation of the cross-environment covariance structure. Under the null hypothesis that the model is correctly specified, $\delta / \operatorname{SE}(\delta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n \to \infty$.

The full identification picture links the abstract objects of the channel framework to observable data through a chain of four biological interpretations:

1
Fitness landscape geometry
The fitness surface $\ell(z, u)$ has gradient $b_0$ and Hessian $H_0$ at the reference environment, and these shift with the environment at rates $b_u$ and $H_u$. This is the abstract theoretical layer — the objects that Papers 1 and 2 are written in.
2
Population-level variances
Under Gaussian closure, the fitness landscape geometry projects onto observable population-level variances: $V_\text{lin} = b_0^\top G b_0$, $V_\text{quad} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_0 G)^2]$, $V_{\text{lin,GEI}} = b_u^\top G b_u$, $V_{\text{quad,GEI}} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$. This is the statistical layer where estimation becomes possible.
3
Reaction norm coefficients
The population-level variances equal the variance components of individual genotype reaction norm parameters: $V_{\text{lin,GEI}} = \text{Var}[\beta_i]$, $V_{\text{quad,GEI}} = \text{Var}[\gamma_i]$. This is the estimation layer — REML provides these directly from a genomic mixed model.
4
MET phenotypes and genomic data
The reaction norm variance components come from fitting the quadratic mixed model to observed $y_{ij}$ using the genomic relationship matrix $\hat{G}$. This is the data layer — standard breeding program outputs with no new experiments required.
