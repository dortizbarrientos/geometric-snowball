$\hat{G}$ — the additive genetic covariance matrix

The additive genetic covariance matrix $G$ is the central object of quantitative genetics. It tells you two things at once: how much heritable variation exists for each trait, and how strongly that variation is correlated across traits.

If you are studying a single trait — say, grain yield — $G$ collapses to a single number: the additive genetic variance $\sigma^2_A$. This is the variance that selection actually acts on, stripped of dominance, epistasis, and environmental noise. It is $\sigma^2_A$ that enters Lande's response equation, Fisher's fundamental theorem, and the additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g$.

With multiple traits, $G$ becomes a matrix. Off-diagonal entries measure genetic correlations: do lines that yield well also tend to flower early? Those correlations can help or hinder selection, and they are exactly what Schluter's lines of least resistance describe.

Why Ĝ is easy in modern breeding

Before genomic data, estimating $G$ required carefully designed pedigrees and large families. Today, a genomic relationship matrix $\hat{G} = MM^\top / (2\sum p_k q_k)$ can be computed from SNP arrays for any set of lines — no pedigree required. This is now routine in any program that genotypes its lines.

Every heritable phenotypic difference between two plants traces back to additive allele substitution effects at loci scattered across the genome. The additive genetic covariance between two traits $z_1$ and $z_2$ is:

Biological meaning of off-diagonal entries

A positive $G_{12}$ means that alleles which increase $z_1$ tend, on average, to also increase $z_2$ — either because of pleiotropy (one gene, two effects) or linkage disequilibrium (two genes that travel together). Breeding programs routinely find that selection for yield inadvertently shifts flowering time, protein content, or disease resistance, precisely because $G$ is not diagonal.

In the channel framework, $G$ plays a structural role: it defines the neighbourhood of trait space that the population actually samples. A population with large $G_{11}$ explores a wide range of $z_1$ values in every generation, making it sensitive to both the gradient and the curvature of the fitness surface along that axis. This is why $V_\text{quad} \propto G^2$: the quadratic variance grows faster than the linear variance as the covariance cloud expands.

Geometrically, $G$ defines an ellipsoid in trait space — the covariance cloud. Its principal axes are the eigenvectors of $G$; their lengths are proportional to the square roots of the eigenvalues. The population samples the fitness surface through this ellipsoid.

The genomic relationship matrix is computed as:

Genomic relationship matrix (VanRaden 2008)
$$\hat{G} = \frac{M M^\top}{2\sum_{k=1}^p p_k(1-p_k)}$$

where $M$ is the $n \times p$ centred marker matrix with $M_{ik} = m_{ik} - 2p_k$ ($m_{ik} \in \{0,1,2\}$ allele counts at SNP $k$ for line $i$), and $p_k$ is the allele frequency of the reference allele at locus $k$.

In the channel framework, $\hat{G}$ enters in two places: the linear variance $V_\text{lin} = b^\top \hat{G} b$ and the quadratic variance $V_\text{quad} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(\hat{H}\hat{G})^2]$. The one-copy vs. two-copy asymmetry between these expressions is why variance compression deepens a channel: halving $G$ halves $V_\text{lin}$ but quarters $V_\text{quad}$.

$\hat{b}_0$ — the local fitness gradient

The fitness gradient $b$ is the direction in trait space that selection is currently pushing the population. It answers the question: if you could increase any trait by one unit, which trait would most increase fitness?

In evolutionary biology, this is estimated as the selection gradient $\beta$ from Lande and Arnold's (1983) regression of relative fitness on trait values. It is the slope of the fitness surface at the current population mean — the steepest direction uphill on the local landscape.

In plant breeding, the fitness gradient has a direct interpretation: it is the direction of greatest return to selection. Lines with large $b^\top \Delta z_i$ — those whose breeding values are best aligned with $b$ — will have the highest predicted breeding values for fitness. This is precisely what GBLUP and genomic selection are estimating.

Why b matters for the channel

The linear fitness variance $V_\text{lin} = b^\top G b$ depends on both the gradient and the covariance structure. A large gradient aligned with a direction of abundant genetic variance produces a deep channel. The same gradient pointing toward a direction of low variance gives a shallow channel — and vice versa.

The Lande-Arnold selection gradient has a precise biological interpretation. Under the animal model with weak selection, the expected per-generation change in the mean breeding value is:

Lande's response equation
$$\Delta\bar{z} = G\,b$$

Selection pushes the mean in the direction $b$, but the actual evolutionary response follows $Gb$ — the gradient pre-multiplied by the covariance matrix. This is the genetic lines of least resistance: evolution is biased toward directions of large genetic variance regardless of the gradient direction. The channel framework quantifies when this bias is strong enough to matter ($\mathcal{A}_g$ low) and when the gradient dominates ($\mathcal{A}_g$ high).

In a field trial, $b$ is estimated by regressing relative fitness (e.g. relative yield, relative seed number, relative survival) on centred breeding value estimates (GBLUP predictions). The regression coefficients are the components of $\hat{b}_0$.

The fitness gradient $b$ is the normal vector to the tangent plane of the fitness surface at the current population mean. It points uphill. The magnitude $\|b\|$ measures how steep the fitness surface is at that location.

The Lande-Arnold regression is fitted as an ordinary least squares model:

Lande-Arnold selection gradient (linear)
$$w_i = \alpha + \hat{b}_0^\top \Delta\hat{z}_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \hat{b}_0 = \bigl(\hat{Z}^\top \hat{Z}\bigr)^{-1}\hat{Z}^\top \mathbf{w}$$

where $w_i = W_i / \bar{W}$ is the relative fitness of line $i$, $\Delta\hat{z}_i = \hat{z}_i - \bar{\hat{z}}$ is the centred GBLUP prediction, and $\hat{Z}$ is the design matrix of centred breeding values.

In practice, one common breeding route is to regress relative fitness on genomic BLUP (GBLUP) predictions of breeding values — the best linear unbiased predictors from the mixed model $\mathbf{y} = X\tau + Z_g g + \varepsilon$ with $g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_g \hat{G})$. This gives an empirical estimate of the local gradient at the reference environment. The exact scaling depends on how fitness is defined and standardised, so $\hat{b}_0$ should be interpreted as an estimated local selection gradient on the chosen analysis scale.

Practical sample size requirements

Kingsolver et al. (2001) found that $n \geq 150$–200 individuals are typically needed for a reliable estimate of $b$ in natural populations. In breeding programs with genomic BLUP predictions, sample sizes of 200–500 lines per environment are standard and usually sufficient. Estimating $H$ (Section 3) requires substantially more.

3

$\hat{H}_0$ — the local fitness Hessian

The fitness Hessian $H$ is the matrix of second derivatives of the fitness surface. Where the gradient $b$ tells you which direction is uphill, the Hessian tells you how the slope changes as you move: whether the surface bends toward a peak, curves away from it, or is flat in some directions and sharply curved in others.

For a plant breeder, $H$ is the object that distinguishes a smooth hill (easy to predict) from a fitness surface with ridges, valleys, and saddle points (hard to predict). A negative definite $H$ means the population is near a fitness peak — the gradient collapses as the optimum is approached. This is Act II of the channel dynamics.

The estimation challenge

Estimating $H$ reliably requires large samples and carefully designed experiments. Second-order regression coefficients are noisy: the standard error of a quadratic coefficient is roughly twice that of the linear coefficient at the same sample size. This is why the companion paper notes that "estimating $H$ requires more data than estimating $b$." In practice, long-term multi-cycle breeding datasets provide the sample sizes needed.

However — and this is the key insight of the companion paper — you do not always need to estimate $H_0$ directly. When the fitness surface shifts with the environment, the environmental sensitivity of the Hessian, $H_u$, is encoded in the quadratic G×E variance $\sigma^2_\gamma$ from a reaction norm model. App 4 develops this fully.

In the Lande-Arnold framework, the Hessian corresponds to the $\gamma$ matrix of nonlinear (quadratic) selection gradients. Its diagonal elements measure stabilising or disruptive selection on each trait separately; off-diagonal elements measure correlational selection — fitness consequences of particular trait combinations, not just individual trait values.

$H_{ii} < 0$ Stabilising selection on trait $i$: fitness peaks at the current mean, declining on both sides. Common near an adaptive optimum.
$H_{ii} > 0$ Disruptive selection on trait $i$: fitness is lowest at the mean and higher at both extremes. Rare but biologically important in polymorphic systems.
$H_{ij} \neq 0$ Correlational selection: the joint combination of traits $i$ and $j$ matters beyond the sum of their individual effects. This is the signature of epistasis at the phenotypic level.

When $H$ is strongly negative definite and the population is close to the optimum, the gradient $b$ collapses (as $b = Hd \approx 0$ when $d \to 0$ in the moving-optimum model) and $\mathcal{A}_g \to 0$. Directional selection ceases; what remains is stabilising selection — curvature without a gradient. This is precisely why a population near its fitness optimum is less additively predictable, not more.

The local curvature can be estimated by fitting a second-order regression of log-fitness, or another suitably transformed fitness measure, on centred breeding values. This is the multivariate quadratic approximation used in the paper:

Second-order local regression
$$\ell_i \approx \alpha + \hat{b}_0^\top \Delta\hat{z}_i + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\hat{z}_i^\top \hat{H}_0\,\Delta\hat{z}_i + \varepsilon_i$$

If the regression is fitted directly on the local log-fitness scale, the quadratic coefficient matrix is the Hessian estimate $\hat{H}_0$ itself. If fitness is analysed on another scale, the mapping to $\hat{H}_0$ depends on the chosen link function and the exact statistical model, so that conversion should be made with care.

Interpretation

The safe point for this app is simple: $\hat{H}_0$ is the fitted local curvature matrix. Its diagonal terms describe stabilising or disruptive selection on single traits; its off-diagonal terms describe correlational curvature across traits. Once $\hat{H}_0$ is in hand, the quadratic variance is computed as $V_\text{quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(\hat{H}_0\hat{G})^2]$.

The variance of $V_\text{quad}$ depends on the quality of both $\hat{H}_0$ and $\hat{G}$. In practice, estimating curvature is materially harder than estimating the gradient, so larger datasets or pooled multi-environment information are usually needed.

Step 1 — Estimate GBLUP breeding values. Fit the genomic mixed model:

Genomic mixed model
$$\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\tau} + Z_g\mathbf{g} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \quad \mathbf{g} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma^2_g \hat{G})$$
Software

ASReml-R, rrBLUP, sommer, BGLR, or any GBLUP solver. Output: $\hat{g}_i$ for each line $i$, and $\hat{G}$ from marker data.

Step 2 — Estimate the linear selection gradient $\hat{b}_0$. Regress relative fitness on centred GBLUP predictions:

Linear selection gradient
$$w_i = \alpha + \hat{b}_0^\top \Delta\hat{g}_i + \varepsilon_i$$
Practical note

Use ridge regression if the number of traits exceeds $\sim$10, to stabilise the estimates. In breeding programs, "traits" here means the underlying quantitative genetic factors, not all measured phenotypes.

Step 3 — Estimate local curvature. Extend the local regression to include all pairwise quadratic terms in the centred breeding values:

Second-order local regression
$$\ell_i \approx \alpha + \hat{b}_0^\top \Delta\hat{g}_i + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\hat{g}_i^\top \hat{H}_0\,\Delta\hat{g}_i + \varepsilon_i$$
Sample size warning

With $n$ traits, the symmetric curvature matrix has $n(n+1)/2$ unique entries. For 5 traits, that is 15 quadratic coefficients. Reliable estimation therefore needs substantially more data than the linear step, and pooling information across environments is often the practical way forward.

Step 4 — Interpret the fitted curvature matrix.

What to check

Diagonal entries tell you about single-trait curvature; off-diagonal entries tell you about correlational curvature. A matrix that is mostly negative on the diagonal and not too noisy off the diagonal is consistent with local stabilising selection. The exact statistical interpretation depends on whether you fitted log-fitness directly or another transformed response.

Step 5 — Compute the channel diagnostics.

From building blocks to diagnostics
$$V_\text{lin} = \hat{b}_0^\top \hat{G}\,\hat{b}_0, \quad V_\text{quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\!\bigl[(\hat{H}_0\hat{G})^2\bigr], \quad \hat{\mathcal{A}}_g = \frac{V_\text{lin}}{V_\text{lin} + V_\text{quad}}$$
What you now have

A single number $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g \in (0,1)$ measuring how reliably additive the current selection environment is. Values above your threshold $\alpha$ mean the linear approximation is doing most of the predictive work. App 5 shows how to set $\alpha$ and interpret trends in $r(t)$.

Putting it together — what the three objects tell a breeder

Each of the three building blocks answers a different question about the current state of a breeding program:

Ĝ alone How much heritable variation is there, and in which directions? Ĝ answers the standard question of quantitative genetics but says nothing about what selection is doing with that variation.
Ĝ + b̂₀ How much of the heritable variation is currently aligned with selection? This gives $V_\text{lin} = \hat{b}_0^\top \hat{G}\hat{b}_0$ — Fisher's first-order fitness increase. Lande's equation predicts evolutionary response from these two objects alone.
Ĝ + b̂₀ + Ĥ₀ How much of the remaining heritable variation is exposed to curvature — to nonlinear selection that the linear model misses? This gives $V_\text{quad}$ and therefore $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g$. Only with all three objects can you assess whether the linear model is a reliable approximation or a dangerous one.
The hierarchy of building blocks

Every breeding program already estimates Ĝ. Most programs with long-term records can estimate b̂₀. Estimating Ĥ₀ reliably requires larger datasets, but the MET structural model (Apps 3–4) provides an alternative route: $H_u$ — the Hessian's environmental sensitivity — is recoverable from reaction norm curvature variance without a direct quadratic regression.

From the perspective of the channel transport laws, the three building blocks determine the instantaneous rate at which $\mathcal{A}_g$ is changing. Recall from Paper 2:

Rate diagnostic from building blocks
$$r_\text{cov} = \frac{\hat{b}_0^\top \dot{\hat{G}}\,\hat{b}_0}{V_\text{lin}} - \frac{\operatorname{tr}[\hat{H}_0\hat{G}\hat{H}_0\dot{\hat{G}}]}{V_\text{quad}}$$

A breeding program under strong directional selection has $\dot{\hat{G}} \approx \hat{G}\hat{H}_0\hat{G}$ (the selection response term from the G-matrix evolution equation). Substituting:

Selection component of rate diagnostic
$$r_\text{sel} = \frac{\hat{b}_0^\top \hat{G}\hat{H}_0\hat{G}\hat{b}_0}{V_\text{lin}} - \frac{\operatorname{tr}[(\hat{H}_0\hat{G})^3]}{V_\text{quad}}$$

When $\hat{H}_0$ is negative definite (stabilising selection), the first term of $r_\text{sel}$ is negative (directional variance is eroding) and the second term is also negative (curvature variance is eroding). The net sign depends on which erodes faster — and the scaling asymmetry says $V_\text{quad}$ erodes faster, so $r_\text{sel}$ tends to be positive. Directional selection deepens channels. This is the core mechanistic insight of the framework, now written in terms of the estimated building blocks.

In the one-trait moving-optimum model ($b = sd$, $H = -s$), the three building blocks reduce to three scalars and the channel diagnostics have closed-form expressions:

One-trait closed-form diagnostics
$$V_\text{lin} = s^2 d^2 G, \quad V_\text{quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}s^2 G^2, \quad \hat{\mathcal{A}}_g = \frac{d^2}{d^2 + G/2}$$

These are Equations (S34)–(S36) of Paper 1's SI. They show three things simultaneously:

  • $\mathcal{A}_g$ does not depend on the curvature strength $s$ — it cancels from numerator and denominator. Only the ratio of lag to variance matters.
  • Large genetic variance $G$ lowers $\mathcal{A}_g$, not because it makes the biology more complex, but because the covariance cloud samples more curvature (the $G^2$ term in $V_\text{quad}$).
  • Large lag $d$ raises $\mathcal{A}_g$, because the directional signal $b = sd$ is large relative to the curvature exposure.

The multivariate generalisation replaces $d^2$ with $b^\top G^{-1} b \cdot \|b\|^{-2}$ (a measure of gradient-covariance alignment) and $G/2$ with $\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(HG)^2]/\|b\|^4$ (the scaled curvature load). App 4 derives the full separation in the MET context.
