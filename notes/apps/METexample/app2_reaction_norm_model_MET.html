From Finlay–Wilkinson to the quadratic model

In 1963, Finlay and Wilkinson proposed the simplest possible model for how genotypes respond to varying environments: each genotype has a fixed intercept (its average performance) and a slope (how much better or worse it does as the environment improves). That model has become one of the most used tools in plant breeding. It works well when genotype responses are roughly linear across environmental conditions.

The channel framework requires one extension: a quadratic term $\gamma_i \Delta u_j^2$. This captures the curvature of the response — whether a genotype's advantage accelerates, decelerates, or reverses across the environmental range. Without this term, the fitness Hessian $H_u$ is invisible to the model, and we cannot compute $V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$ or $r_\text{env}$.

The extension is minor, the payoff is major

Adding a single quadratic term to a standard reaction norm model — something any mixed model software handles — opens the door to the full channel diagnostic. The curvature variance $\sigma^2_\gamma = \text{Var}[\gamma_i]$ is the only new quantity needed beyond what Finlay-Wilkinson already estimates.

The reaction norm is the phenotypic expression of a genotype across environments. A steep positive $\beta_i$ means the genotype is environmentally responsive: it improves strongly as conditions become more favourable. In plant breeding, these are often called high-input or highly responsive varieties. A flat $\beta_i \approx 0$ is an environmentally stable or buffered genotype — more predictable across environments, but not necessarily the highest performer.

The curvature $\gamma_i$ captures something more subtle:

  • Negative $\gamma_i$ (concave-down): performance peaks at intermediate environments and falls at extremes. This is a stress-sensitive generalist — good under moderate conditions, poor under both drought and waterlogging.
  • Positive $\gamma_i$ (concave-up): performance is lowest at the middle and rises toward extremes. A rare but real pattern in specialist varieties adapted to extreme conditions.
  • $\gamma_i \approx 0$: the Finlay–Wilkinson linear approximation holds. The nonlinear GEI is negligible for this genotype.
Connection to Paper 2

In the channel transport law $\dot{H} = T[\mathbf{G}b] + H_u \dot{u}$, the term $H_u \dot{u}$ is the rate at which the fitness Hessian changes as the environment shifts. The curvature variance $\sigma^2_\gamma = \text{Var}[\gamma_i] = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$ measures the population-level exposure to that Hessian drift. High $\sigma^2_\gamma$ means the population spans a wide range of curvature responses — some genotypes accelerating, others decelerating — which is the signature of a strongly curved fitness landscape along the environmental axis.

The quadratic reaction norm model specifies:

Quadratic reaction norm mixed model
$$y_{ij} = \mu_j + \alpha_i + \beta_i\,\Delta u_j + \gamma_i\,\Delta u_j^2 + \varepsilon_{ij}$$

where $\mu_j$ is a fixed environment main effect, $\Delta u_j = u_j - u_0$ is the centred environmental state, and $(\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i)$ are random genotype-specific coefficients with joint distribution:

Random effects distribution
$$\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_i\\\beta_i\\\gamma_i\end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\; \Phi \otimes \hat{G}\right), \quad \Phi = \begin{pmatrix}\phi_{\alpha\alpha}&\phi_{\alpha\beta}&\phi_{\alpha\gamma}\\\phi_{\alpha\beta}&\phi_{\beta\beta}&\phi_{\beta\gamma}\\\phi_{\alpha\gamma}&\phi_{\beta\gamma}&\phi_{\gamma\gamma}\end{pmatrix}$$

The Kronecker structure $\Phi \otimes \hat{G}$ means that the same genetic relatedness governs all three response parameters — a genetically related pair of lines will tend to have similar intercepts, similar slopes, and similar curvatures. The $3 \times 3$ matrix $\Phi$ of scalar variance components is what the mixed model estimates. In the channel framework, these quantities can then be interpreted as empirical counterparts of the key GEI objects:

Bridge from $\Phi$ to the channel framework
$$\phi_{\beta\beta}\;\text{tracks the linear GEI variance}, \qquad \phi_{\gamma\gamma}\;\text{tracks the nonlinear GEI variance}$$ $$\phi_{\alpha\beta}\;\text{tracks how mean performance covaries with environmental sensitivity}$$

Under the Gaussian local approximation used in the companion theory, these are identified with $b_u^\top G b_u$, $\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$, and $b_0^\top G b_u$, respectively. The important teaching point here is that standard reaction norm mixed models are estimating the quantities needed to separate linear from nonlinear GEI.

2

Variance components and what they measure

The mixed model produces three variance components as its primary output. Each answers a distinct question about the population's genetic architecture of environmental response:

σ²α
Intercept variance
How much do genotypes differ in average performance across all environments? This is the classic genetic variance — the signal that additive genomic prediction captures at the reference environment.
σ²β
Slope variance = $V_{\text{lin,GEI}}$
How much do genotypes differ in responsiveness? High $\sigma^2_\beta$ means some lines gain a lot from good conditions while others are indifferent. This is linear G×E — predictable from genomic data.
σ²γ
Curvature variance = $V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$
How much do genotypes differ in nonlinear response — whether their advantage accelerates or reverses across the range? High $\sigma^2_\gamma$ signals a curved fitness surface; this is the hardest G×E to predict.
σαβ
Intercept–slope covariance
Do high-performing lines also tend to be more responsive to the environment? Positive $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ means yes — a common pattern in high-input varieties. This enters the mixed term $b_0^\top G b_u$ in $r_\text{env}$.
The GEI additivity index

The ratio $\mathcal{A}_g^{(\text{GEI})} = \sigma^2_\beta / (\sigma^2_\beta + \sigma^2_\gamma)$ measures whether G×E is predominantly linear (predictable) or predominantly nonlinear (chaotic). When it is high, Finlay–Wilkinson is a good model for the data. When it is low, something more complex — curvature of the fitness surface with respect to the environment — is driving genotype rankings.

Each variance component maps onto a distinct biological mechanism:

  • $\sigma^2_\alpha$ reflects genetic differences in the baseline growth programme — alleles affecting yield potential independent of conditions. These are the alleles standard GWAS identifies under controlled environments.
  • $\sigma^2_\beta$ reflects genetic differences in plasticity — alleles that regulate responses to environmental signals (water, temperature, light). QTL for $\sigma^2_\beta$ map to regulatory pathways: drought-response transcription factors, osmotic adjustment genes, temperature-sensing receptors.
  • $\sigma^2_\gamma$ reflects genetic differences in the curvature of plastic response. High $\sigma^2_\gamma$ implies genotypes that respond optimally to different environmental intensities — classic niche specialisation at the genetic level. QTL for $\sigma^2_\gamma$ are less studied but are expected to map to genes controlling the sensitivity of plastic responses themselves (meta-plasticity).
  • $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ reflects whether high-yielding alleles and high-responsiveness alleles are linked (positive covariance) or in opposition (negative covariance). Negative $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ creates a stability–performance tradeoff: the most stable genotypes are not the highest performers. This tradeoff is well-documented in crop breeding and is a direct consequence of the geometry of $b_0^\top G b_u$.

Within the Gaussian local approximation used in the companion theory, the population-level variance of each coefficient can be written in terms of the channel objects:

Identification under the local Gaussian approximation
$$\phi_{\beta\beta} = \operatorname{Var}_z[\beta_i] = \operatorname{Var}_z[b_u^\top \Delta z_i] = b_u^\top G\, b_u$$ $$\phi_{\gamma\gamma} = \operatorname{Var}_z[\gamma_i] = \operatorname{Var}_z\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta z_i^\top H_u \Delta z_i\right] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$$ $$\phi_{\alpha\beta} = \operatorname{Cov}_z[\alpha_i,\beta_i] = \operatorname{Cov}_z[b_0^\top \Delta z_i,\; b_u^\top \Delta z_i] = b_0^\top G\, b_u$$

The second identification uses the Isserlis/Wick theorem for fourth moments of a centred Gaussian (the same result used in the SI appendix of the paper): for any symmetric matrix $A$,

Isserlis identity (applied)
$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta z^\top A\,\Delta z\right] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(AG)^2]$$

With $A = H_u$, this gives $\phi_{\gamma\gamma} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2] = V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$ — the exact same Isserlis formula that gives $V_{\text{quad}} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_0 G)^2]$ for the reference environment. The variance of the curvature coefficient is the quadratic GEI variance. This is the structural result that makes the whole estimation programme work.

3

Estimation in practice

Fitting the quadratic reaction norm model requires a mixed model solver that supports random regression — the ability to model random effects as functions of a covariate (here, $\Delta u_j$). All major quantitative genetics software packages support this.

The key practical decisions are:

  • How to define $u_j$: a measured environmental covariate (e.g. drought stress index), a derived score (PCA of weather variables), or a data-driven estimate (FA(1) factor score from the MET). The first is most interpretable for $r_\text{env}$; the last requires no external data.
  • How many environments: a minimum of 3 distinct $u_j$ values is needed to identify $\phi_{\alpha\alpha}$, $\phi_{\beta\beta}$, and $\phi_{\gamma\gamma}$. In practice, $E \geq 6$–8 environments at diverse conditions gives stable estimates of $\phi_{\gamma\gamma}$.
  • How many lines: $\phi_{\gamma\gamma}$ is the most demanding to estimate precisely. Simulations suggest $n \geq 200$ lines with $E \geq 6$ environments provides acceptable standard errors for $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$.
Software

ASReml-R (rr() function for random regression), sommer (R package, vsr(leg())), or breedR. For large datasets, ASReml standalone is fastest. The legendre() polynomial basis in ASReml naturally implements the quadratic model when truncated at order 2.

In a long-term breeding program, the reaction norm model should be refitted each cycle as new lines enter the program and new environments accumulate. The key quantities to track longitudinally are:

  • $\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}$ over cycles: if directional selection for yield is strong, this should decline as lines converge on the optimal response slope — an Act II channel-deepening signal.
  • $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ over cycles: persistent high values indicate a genuinely curved fitness landscape — the nonlinear G×E is not being eroded by selection. This is a warning sign that the reference environment model is missing important structure.
  • $\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}$ over cycles: a shift from positive to negative signals a growing stability–performance tradeoff, which often appears when a breeding program has pushed yield potential close to the local optimum under high-input conditions.

These longitudinal trends are exactly the inputs to the rate diagnostic $r_\text{env}$, computed from the formula in App 11.

The ASReml code for the quadratic random regression model is:

ASReml-R specification (pseudocode)
asreml(
  fixed  = yield ~ env,
  random = ~ rr(du, 2):vm(line, Ghat),
  data   = met_data
)

where du is the centred environmental covariate $\Delta u_j$, 2 specifies a Legendre polynomial of order 2 (giving intercept, slope, quadratic terms), and vm(line, Ghat) applies the genomic relationship matrix $\hat{G}$ as the variance model for line effects.

The sommer equivalent:

sommer specification
mmer(
  yield ~ env,
  random = ~ vsr(leg(du, 2), line, Gvar = Ghat),
  data   = met_data
)

The estimated variance-covariance matrix of random regression coefficients, $\hat{\Phi}$, is extracted from the model output. Its diagonal entries $\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\alpha}$, $\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}$, $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ are the variance components. The off-diagonal $\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta}$ is the intercept-slope covariance. All four enter the channel diagnostics in App 11.

Legendre polynomial note

ASReml's rr(du, k) uses orthogonal Legendre polynomials on $[-1, 1]$, not the raw $\{1, \Delta u, \Delta u^2\}$ basis. The estimated $\hat{\Phi}$ must be back-transformed to the raw polynomial basis before plugging into the identification formulae. App 9 shows this transformation explicitly.

4

Reading $\hat{\Phi}$ as a breeding diagnostic

Once $\hat{\Phi}$ is estimated, four breeding-relevant questions have immediate answers:

  1. Is the G×E predominantly linear? Compute $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})} = \hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} / (\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma})$. Values above 0.70 mean Finlay-Wilkinson models the G×E adequately. Values below 0.50 mean the curvature term is explaining most of the genotype-by-environment signal — genomic prediction across environments will be unreliable without modelling $\gamma_i$.
  2. Is there a stability–performance tradeoff? If $\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta} < 0$, selecting for high mean performance will select against stability — and vice versa. The breeder must decide which to prioritise, or seek lines with both high $\alpha_i$ and low $|\beta_i|$ (which exist only if $\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta}$ is not strongly negative).
  3. Which individual lines are most stable? Sort predicted $\hat{\beta}_i$ values. Lines closest to zero are most stable across the environmental range. Lines with large positive $\hat{\beta}_i$ are high-input responsive varieties.
  4. What will climate change do to genomic prediction reliability? If climate shifts the environmental mean by $\dot{u}$ units per cycle, $r_\text{env} \propto \hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta} \cdot \dot{u}$. App 11 computes this precisely.

The $\hat{\Phi}$ matrix is a compact summary of the population's genetic architecture of plasticity. In evolutionary terms, it is the plasticity analogue of the $G$ matrix — it describes how heritable variation in environmental responsiveness is structured.

A key evolutionary result follows immediately from the identification: if $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$ is large, then the fitness landscape is genuinely curved along the environmental axis — there is no single optimal response strategy. The population contains genetic variation for which environment a genotype performs best in. Natural selection will tend to fix locally adapted specialists rather than a universal generalist. From a breeding perspective, this means that variety recommendations must be environment-specific, and that genomic prediction models trained in one environment set will transfer poorly to novel environments.

Act III and $\hat{\Phi}$

In Paper 2's Act III scenario — channel exit from gradient rotation — the environmental shift $\dot{u}$ drives $r_\text{env} \ll 0$. In terms of $\hat{\Phi}$, this happens when $\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta}$ is small or negative (mean performance and slope are decoupled) while $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ is substantial. The channel erodes because the fitness gradient rotates away from the major axis of $G$ faster than the population can track it. $\hat{\Phi}$ gives early warning of this before genomic prediction accuracy falls.

This app stops at the variance-component stage. The full reconstruction of $\hat{r}_{\text{env}}$ needs the structural covariance model developed in the next apps, where the cross-environment covariance surface is fitted explicitly and the mixed curvature term is separated cleanly. For now, the safe interpretation is this:

What $\hat{\Phi}$ already tells you
$$\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\mathrm{GEI})} = \frac{\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}}{\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}+\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}}$$

A large $\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}$ relative to $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ means the GEI is mainly linear and therefore more predictable. A large $\hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$ means curvature is important and the environmental response surface is genuinely nonlinear. The sign of $\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta}$ then tells you whether high-performing lines also tend to be more environmentally responsive or whether there is a stability–performance tradeoff. The later apps combine these pieces into the full diagnostic pipeline.
