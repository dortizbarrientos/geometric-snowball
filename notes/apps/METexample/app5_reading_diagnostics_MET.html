The channel trajectory simulator

The channel framework from Papers 1 and 2 describes how additive predictability — $\mathcal{A}_g(t)$ — evolves over time as selection acts, the environment shifts, and variance enters or leaves the population. The simulator below brings this to life for the one-trait moving-optimum model, in which all parameters are estimable from a MET.

Three phases — Acts I, II, and III — produce qualitatively distinct signatures in both $\mathcal{A}_g(t)$ and $r(t)$. The key insight is that $r(t)$ gives warning before $\mathcal{A}_g(t)$ crosses the threshold. A program that monitors only prediction accuracy will detect channel exit only after it has happened. One that monitors $r(t)$ detects it while there is still time to intervene.

Act structure

The three acts map onto recognisable phases in real breeding programs and natural populations:

Act I — Channel open
Directional selection, open channel
Âg > α, r̂(t) > 0
The optimum is moving, selection is directional, the gradient is large relative to the covariance cloud. Genomic prediction is reliable. Lande's equation describes response accurately. This is the early phase of adaptation to a new environment — or the opening cycles of a selection program in a new target environment.
Act II — Optimum approached
Stabilising selection, channel closing
Âg > α, r̂(t) < 0
The population has tracked the optimum; the lag $d$ has shrunk and the gradient $b = sd$ is collapsing. Additive prediction still works today — the channel is open — but $r(t) < 0$ warns of impending exit. In a breeding program: genomic models trained now will be less accurate in future cycles. Intervention options: widen the program, introduce new germplasm, or redefine the selection target.
Act III — Gradient rotation
Environmental change, silent exit
r̂_env ≪ 0, r̂_cov ≥ 0
Climate change rotates the fitness gradient away from the major axis of G. No genetic variance is lost; curvature is unchanged. Yet $\mathcal{A}_g$ falls because the gradient now projects poorly onto the covariance structure. Standard genomic selection fails silently. Diagnosis: the sign pattern $r_\text{env} \ll 0$, $r_\text{cov} \geq 0$ separates Act III from Act II. Intervention: targeted introgression of variance aligned with the new gradient direction.
Re-entry
Channel recovery
Âg < α, r̂(t) > 0 sustained
After channel exit, recovery occurs when $r(t) > 0$ for long enough that $\mathcal{L}(t)$ rises back above $\mathcal{L}_\alpha$. In practice: new genetic variance aligned with the current gradient (from introgression or recombination), or a new optimum shift that regenerates directional signal. Monitor $\mathcal{L}(t)$ — the logit integral of $r(t)$ — as the leading indicator of re-entry.

The simulator above shows the one-trait model. Its four panels follow the variables from Paper 2 exactly: lag and variance (state variables), logit trajectory $\mathcal{L}(t)$, additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g(t)$, and the rate diagnostic $r(t)$ decomposed by process. Adjust all seven parameters and watch how the three-act structure changes.

The one-trait closed-form system (Paper 2, §S6) used in the simulator:

One-trait state equations (per generation)
$$\dot{d} = \dot\theta - Gsd, \qquad \dot{G} = -sG^2 - \lambda_\text{rec}(G - G^*) + m - \frac{G}{2N_e}$$
Closed-form diagnostics
$$\mathcal{A}_g = \frac{d^2}{d^2 + G/2}, \qquad r = \frac{2\dot\theta}{d} - sG + \lambda_\text{rec}\!\left(1 - \frac{G^*}{G}\right) - \frac{m}{G} + \frac{1}{2N_e}$$

$r(t)$ warns before $\mathcal{A}_g(t)$ crosses $\alpha$. The lead time — how many generations of warning $r(t) < 0$ provides — depends on how far above $\alpha$ the channel currently sits and how negative $r$ is. The logit trajectory $\mathcal{L}(t) = \mathcal{L}(0) + \int_0^t r(s)\,ds$ integrates this warning continuously.

Separating Act II from Act III in real data

Both Act II and Act III produce a falling $\mathcal{A}_g(t)$ and eventually channel exit. They look identical in raw prediction accuracy data. But they require opposite interventions:

  • Act II requires redesigning the selection target — finding new challenges, new environments, new traits to push against. The channel closed because selection succeeded; the fix is to reopen the gap.
  • Act III requires redesigning the genetic resource — introducing variance aligned with the new gradient direction. The channel closed because the environment moved the selection pressure away from where genetic variation is concentrated; the fix is to rotate the covariance cloud.

Applying the wrong intervention wastes cycles. The rate diagnostic decomposition separates the two before the channel exits.

In Darwin's finches on Daphne Major, both acts occurred within a decade:

  • 1977–1984 (Act I → II): The 1977 drought imposed a steep directional gradient on beak depth. Beak depth shifted nearly one full standard deviation in one generation — Act I in action. Through the early 1980s, the population tracked the new optimum, the lag shrank, and the channel narrowed — Act II. Prediction accuracy for beak depth response would have been declining throughout the 1980s even as phenotypic change continued.
  • 1983 El Niño (Act III onset): Heavy rains rotated the seed community toward small soft seeds, rotating the fitness gradient away from deep beaks toward wider, shorter beaks. No genetic variance was lost. But the gradient was now pointing in a direction poorly aligned with the covariance structure shaped by years of depth-oriented selection. Act III: $r_\text{env} \ll 0$, $r_\text{cov} \geq 0$.

The rate diagnostic would have flagged the distinction in real time: negative $r_\text{env}$ in 1983 versus near-zero $r_\text{env}$ throughout the previous Act II period.

The decomposition of $r(t)$ into mechanistic contributions (Paper 2, §S5) is:

Three-way decomposition
$$r(t) = r_\text{motion} + r_\text{env} + r_\text{cov}$$
Sign pattern diagnostic table
$$\text{Act II}: \quad r_\text{env} \approx 0,\quad r_\text{cov} < 0, \quad r_\text{motion} \approx 0$$ $$\text{Act III}: \quad r_\text{env} \ll 0,\quad r_\text{cov} \geq 0, \quad r_\text{motion} \approx 0 \text{ or } > 0$$

The test statistic for distinguishing the two acts is:

Act separator test
$$T = \frac{|\hat{r}_\text{env}|}{|\hat{r}_\text{env}| + |\hat{r}_\text{cov}|} \in [0,1]$$

$T \approx 0$ indicates Act II (covariance erosion dominant), $T \approx 1$ indicates Act III (environmental rotation dominant). Values in between indicate a mixture, which arises when both gradient rotation and G-matrix compression are occurring simultaneously — common in real breeding programs facing climate change during active selection.

The introgression criterion — which germplasm to bring in

When channel exit is imminent and the diagnosis points to Act III, the intervention is to introduce new genetic variance aligned with the current fitness gradient. But not all introgression is equal. A wide cross that adds variance in the wrong direction — into directions of high curvature — will lower $\mathcal{A}_g$ rather than raise it.

The introgression criterion (Paper 1, §S7) provides the first-order test for whether a donor line's genetic contribution will deepen or harm the additive channel:

Introgression criterion (Paper 1, §S7)
$$\delta\mathcal{A}_g \propto \frac{(\hat{b}^\top v)^2}{\hat{V}_\text{lin}} - \frac{v^\top \hat{H}\hat{G}\hat{H} v}{\hat{V}_\text{quad}}$$

where $v$ is the direction of incoming variance from the donor. The criterion has a clear biological reading: new variance deepens the channel if gradient alignment benefit ($\hat{b}^\top v$)² exceeds curvature exposure cost $v^\top \hat{H}\hat{G}\hat{H} v$. The optimal donor brings variance squarely aligned with the current fitness gradient.

Practical application

For each candidate donor line $i$, compute $v_i$ as the direction of their breeding value deviation from your current population mean. Then evaluate the introgression criterion. Rank donors by $\delta\mathcal{A}_g$. Select the top-ranked donor for the wide cross. This is the channel-framework guide to germplasm bank selection.

The introgression criterion formalises an intuition that experienced breeders often hold but rarely quantify: an exotic line that performs brilliantly in one environment but poorly in your target environment may still deepen your additive channel — if its poor performance arises from directional misalignment rather than nonlinear epistasis. The criterion separates these two causes.

  • A donor with large $(\hat{b}^\top v)^2$ brings variance aligned with the direction selection is currently pushing. This boosts $V_\text{lin}$ without increasing $V_\text{quad}$ proportionally — a channel deepening.
  • A donor with large $v^\top \hat{H}\hat{G}\hat{H} v$ brings variance exposed to curvature — the fitness consequences of its genetics are highly nonlinear in the current population context. This boosts $V_\text{quad}$ disproportionately — a channel narrowing, even if the donor appears "diverse."

The first quantity is easy to estimate from genomic prediction — it is essentially the GEBV (genomic estimated breeding value) of the donor for the current selection index. The second quantity requires $\hat{H}$, which is harder to estimate (App 7) but is approximated by the nonlinear selection gradient analysis.

The first-order change in $\mathcal{A}_g$ from a rank-1 variance input $G^+ = G + \sigma^2_\Delta vv^\top$ (derived in Paper 1, §S7) is:

First-order change in $\mathcal{A}_g$ from introgression
$$\delta\mathcal{A}_g \approx \mathcal{A}_g(1-\mathcal{A}_g)\left[\frac{(\hat{b}^\top v)^2}{\hat{V}_\text{lin}} - \frac{v^\top \hat{H}\hat{G}\hat{H} v}{\hat{V}_\text{quad}}\right]\sigma^2_\Delta$$

The optimal introgression direction maximises $\delta\mathcal{A}_g$ over unit vectors $v$. This is the leading eigenvector of:

Optimal donor direction
$$v^* = \arg\max_{\|v\|=1}\left[\frac{(\hat{b}^\top v)^2}{\hat{V}_\text{lin}} - \frac{\hat{V}_\text{lin}}{\hat{V}_\text{quad}} v^\top \hat{G}^{1/2}\hat{H}^2\hat{G}^{1/2} v\right]$$

When curvature is isotropic ($\hat{H} = -\gamma I$), the optimal direction simplifies to $v^* \propto \hat{G}^{-1/2}\hat{b}$ — new variance should be gradient-aligned in the metric of $\hat{G}^{-1}$, meaning it should be concentrated in the direction of selection weighted inversely by current genetic variance. This is the multivariate generalisation of "introduce variance in the direction you are selecting."

The complete decision framework

A plant breeder who has followed this series through Apps 0–5 can now compute four numbers each breeding cycle from their standard MET and genomic data:

  1. $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g$ — Is additive prediction currently reliable? (Reference environment, App 7)
  2. $\hat{r}(t)$ decomposed — Is reliability improving or eroding, and which process is responsible? (App 8–9)
  3. $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$ — Is G×E across environments predictable? (App 10)
  4. $\hat{r}_\text{env}$ — Is the environmental shift deepening or eroding the channel? (App 8, 11)

The decision tree below maps these four readings to actionable responses. The complete interactive simulator assembles all four into a live dashboard.

The framework converts a familiar set of breeding observations into a mechanistic language. Three recurring situations that breeders recognise immediately now have precise diagnostic signatures:

  • "Genomic prediction accuracy has been declining for three cycles." Check: is $\hat{r}_\text{env} \ll 0$ (climate-driven Act III)? Or is $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g$ declining while $\hat{r}_\text{cov} < 0$ (variance erosion, Act II)? The response differs fundamentally.
  • "Wide crosses improved diversity metrics but made genomic models worse." Check: was the donor's introgression criterion $\delta\mathcal{A}_g < 0$? New variance in directions of high curvature always harms prediction, regardless of how genetically diverse the donor is by standard metrics.
  • "We've been selecting in well-watered trials but now face drought." Check: $\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta}$ from your MET — if positive, responsive lines also yield well in the well-watered reference, and the climate shift will erode $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g$ rapidly (Act III). If negative, your stable lines are already somewhat drought-tolerant and the channel may survive the shift better than expected.

A compact working summary of the four main diagnostics, written in terms of the MET-estimable quantities assembled across the series:

The complete four-diagnostic system
$$\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g = \frac{\hat{V}_\text{lin}^{(0)}}{\hat{c}_{00}} \qquad \hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})} = \frac{\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta}}{\hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}}$$ $$\hat{r}_\text{env} \approx \frac{2\hat{\phi}_{\alpha\beta}}{\hat{c}_{00}\,\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g}\cdot\dot{u} \qquad \hat{r}_\text{cov} = \frac{\hat{b}_0^\top \dot{\hat{G}}\hat{b}_0}{\hat{V}_\text{lin}} - \frac{\operatorname{tr}[\hat{H}_0\hat{G}\hat{H}_0\dot{\hat{G}}]}{\hat{V}_\text{quad}}$$

Together these four numbers constitute a complete first-order audit of the channel's current state and trajectory. They convert the abstract geometry of Papers 1 and 2 into a practical cycle-by-cycle monitoring tool for any breeding program with genomic and MET data.
