What is a Multi-Environment Trial?

A working introduction to the experimental design that underpins practical genomic prediction — written for a geneticist who knows quantitative genetics but has not worked directly with field trials.

Prerequisite Basic quantitative genetics: breeding values, variance, covariance, and selection response
1

The core idea

A multi-environment trial (MET) is exactly what its name promises: the same set of genotypes grown, observed, and measured across multiple environments simultaneously. In wheat, those environments might be ten locations across three rainfall zones. In soybean, they might be five years at the same field under contrasting planting densities. In maize, a combination of drought-stressed and well-watered conditions at a dozen sites.

The MET is the workhorse of plant breeding. Every commercial variety released in the last sixty years passed through something like it. Before a breeder commits to advancing a line, they need to know: does this variety perform well everywhere, or only in the environment where it was bred? The answer to that question shapes the entire structure of a breeding program.

Core question

When does the best genotype in one environment stay the best in another? A MET is the experimental apparatus designed to answer that question at scale.

For a quantitative geneticist, the MET produces something more precise than intuition about "best varieties": it produces a genetic covariance structure across environments. That structure is the raw material for the channel diagnostics derived in the companion paper.

Plants do not experience a single environment. A wheat crop in Queensland encounters different temperatures, soil types, rainfall patterns, and photoperiods depending on where and when it grows. The same genotype can produce vastly different phenotypes across these conditions — a phenomenon called phenotypic plasticity. When genotypes differ in their plastic responses, the result is a genotype-by-environment interaction (G×E).

G×E comes in two broad flavours with different consequences for prediction:

  • Scaling interaction (non-crossover G×E): genotype ranks are preserved across environments, but the differences between genotypes expand or contract. Variety A yields more than Variety B in all environments; the margin just varies. Additive prediction usually survives this kind of G×E.
  • Rank-change interaction (crossover G×E): genotype A outperforms B in dry conditions but underperforms B in wet conditions. Prediction based on one environment becomes unreliable or misleading in another. This is the biologically important case for breeders because the identity of the “best” genotype depends on where the crop is grown.
Key biological insight

Crossover G×E tells us that the response surface is not behaving like one fixed straight plane across environments. The change can come from changing slopes, changing curvature, or both. In the channel framework, the MET helps us separate these possibilities instead of hiding them inside one undifferentiated interaction term.

Adaptive radiations and breeding programs share the same geometry. In both cases, a set of genotypes samples a curved fitness surface across a range of environments. The MET is the controlled version of what natural selection runs continuously in the wild.

Think of the MET as constructing a matrix $Y$ with genotypes as rows and environments as columns. Each entry $y_{ij}$ is the observed phenotype (yield, fitness, trait value) of genotype $i$ in environment $j$.

The geometry of the MET data matrix gives clues about the local response surface. When all rows of $Y$ move in roughly parallel fashion, the same ranking is preserved and the interaction is mostly a matter of scale. When rows cross, the local response to the environment is changing among genotypes. In the later apps, we show how that change can be decomposed into a linear part and a curvature part, and how the additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g$ measures their balance.

The fundamental decomposition of a phenotype observed in a MET is:

Two-way model (basic)
$$y_{ij} = \mu + g_i + e_j + (ge)_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}$$
$\mu$
Grand mean
Overall population mean across all genotypes and environments
$g_i$
Genotype main effect
Mean advantage of genotype $i$ averaged across all environments; the additive breeding value component
$e_j$
Environment main effect
Mean productivity of environment $j$ regardless of which genotype is grown there
$(ge)_{ij}$
G×E interaction
The deviation of genotype $i$ in environment $j$ from what the additive model predicts — the curvature signal
$\varepsilon_{ij}$
Residual error
Measurement noise, plot error, within-environment microvariation

This model is the starting point. The channel framework replaces the opaque $(ge)_{ij}$ term with a structured decomposition in which the G×E signal is resolved into linear GEI (reaction norm slopes, driven by $b_u$) and nonlinear GEI (reaction norm curvatures, driven by $H_u$). The payoff is that $\mathcal{A}_g$ and $r_\text{env}$ become estimable from standard MET variance components. Apps 2–4 in this series derive that resolution in full.

The data structure

A typical plant breeding MET involves:

$n$ genotypes Lines, varieties, or hybrids from the breeding program. Modern METs routinely test 100–500 lines. Advanced genomic trials may include thousands.
$E$ environments Physical locations (sites), years, treatment combinations, or any systematically varied condition. A minimum of $E \geq 3$ is required for the identification results in the companion paper; real METs typically span $E = 10$–50 environments.
$r$ replicates Number of plots per genotype per environment. Even a single replicate per genotype per site (augmented design) is sufficient for variance component estimation if the design is well-balanced.
$u_j$ A measured environmental covariate for environment $j$ — for instance, cumulative drought stress index, mean temperature during grain fill, or days with frost. This is the scalar $u$ in the companion theory.
Genomic data SNP marker genotypes for all $n$ lines, from which the additive genetic relationship matrix $\hat{G}$ is constructed. Modern METs are routinely genotyped at $\geq 10,000$ markers.

The environmental covariate $u_j$ deserves special attention because it is the key link between the MET data and the channel theory. In the companion derivation, $u$ is the scalar state of the environment that determines the shape of the fitness surface — specifically, how the gradient $b$ and Hessian $H$ change as conditions shift.

In practice, $u_j$ can be:

  • A direct physical measurement: cumulative water deficit during a critical developmental window, mean maximum temperature during flowering, average solar radiation during grain fill. These have clear physiological interpretations as drivers of the fitness surface shape.
  • A derived index: the first principal component of a set of environmental covariates (e.g. weather station data from the entire growing season compressed into a single index by PCA or random forest).
  • A statistical estimate: the FA(1) factor score from a factor analytic model fitted to the MET data itself. This data-driven approach does not require external environmental measurements and is often used when physical records are incomplete.
Practical note

The identification of $\mathcal{A}_g$ and $r_\text{env}$ works even when $u_j$ is estimated from the data, at the cost of additional uncertainty in the variance component estimates. App 3 quantifies that additional uncertainty.

The MET produces a three-dimensional data object: an $n \times E$ matrix of observed phenotypes, accompanied by:

  • An $n \times p$ marker matrix $M$ (genotypes × SNPs)
  • An $E$-vector of environmental states $u_1, \ldots, u_E$
  • Optionally, an $E \times q$ matrix of environmental covariates

The two-way mixed model with structured covariance is the standard statistical representation of MET data:

Mixed model for the MET
$$\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\tau} + Z_g \mathbf{g} + Z_{ge}\mathbf{ge} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$$

where $\mathbf{y}$ is the stacked vector of all phenotypic observations, $X$ contains fixed effects (environment means, blocks), $\mathbf{g}$ is a vector of random genotype main effects with $\mathbf{g} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \sigma^2_g \hat{G})$, and $\mathbf{ge}$ is a vector of random G×E effects with covariance structure $\Sigma_{ge} \otimes \hat{G}$.

The key matrix in the channel framework is the $E \times E$ genetic covariance matrix across environments:

Cross-environment genetic covariance (fundamental object)
$$C^{(G)}_{jk} = \text{Cov}_z\!\bigl[g_{ij},\, g_{ik}\bigr], \qquad j,k = 1,\ldots,E$$

This matrix is what a factor analytic (FA) model estimates efficiently from the MET data. In the companion derivation, the structural model for $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ — which says it is a quadratic polynomial in $\Delta u_j$ and $\Delta u_k$ — is the bridge between the raw MET output and the channel diagnostics $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g$ and $\hat{r}_\text{env}$. App 3 develops this fully.

3

What a MET actually estimates

A MET is not just a ranking exercise. With genomic data attached, it estimates three nested layers of genetic information:

Mean performance How much better or worse is each genotype on average across all tested environments? In the channel notation, this is the baseline linear component at the reference environment. It is the ordinary breeding-value part of the story.
Environmental sensitivity Does this genotype perform better as conditions improve (positive slope), or is it robust across conditions (flat slope)? In a reaction norm, this is the linear coefficient $\beta_i$. In the channel framework, its variance across genotypes corresponds to the linear GEI quantity $b_u^\top G b_u$.
Response nonlinearity Does the genotype's advantage accelerate or decelerate across the environmental range — a U-shape or inverted-U? In a simple quadratic reaction norm, this is the coefficient $\gamma_i$. In the channel framework, the variance of this curvature-like component corresponds to the quadratic GEI quantity $\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$ under the identification assumptions developed later in the series.
The punchline

A MET lets us estimate how much G×E is carried by a linear reaction-norm component and how much is carried by a curvature component. In the channel framework, these map onto $V_{\text{lin,GEI}} = b_u^\top G b_u$ and $V_{\text{quad,GEI}} = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$ under the structural assumptions introduced later in the series. Their ratio defines a GEI additivity index, which measures whether the interaction is mostly predictable from slopes or whether curvature is doing a large part of the work.

Genotypes as reaction norms

In the MET, each genotype traces out a reaction norm: the phenotypic trajectory of that genotype as the environment changes along the gradient $u$. Reaction norms can be thought of as the individual-level complement to the population-level channel diagnostics.

A genotype with a steep positive slope $\beta_i$ is environmentally responsive: it capitalises on good conditions but suffers in poor ones. A genotype with $\beta_i \approx 0$ is stable across environments — a breeder's term for low environmental sensitivity. In the channel framework, stability corresponds to small $b_u^\top \Delta z_i$: the genotype's breeding value lies in a direction nearly orthogonal to the environmental gradient of the fitness gradient.

The curvature component $\gamma_i$ captures a subtler but important phenomenon: genotypes that are suppressed by both extreme drought and extreme flooding — optimal at intermediate water levels — display a negative $\gamma_i$. Genotypes that are extreme specialists — thriving only at one end of the environmental range — display a positive $\gamma_i$. This nonlinearity is exactly what makes the Hessian $H_u$ non-zero.

The reaction norm of genotype $i$ across the environmental gradient $u$ defines a curve in the (environment, phenotype) plane. With only school calculus, you can read that curve using three familiar ideas: height, slope, and curvature. The shape of the curve is therefore characterised by three parameters:

The three parameters $(\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i)$ define the reaction norm coordinate of genotype $i$ in a three-dimensional space. The MET, combined with genomic data, estimates the distribution of genotypes in that space — that is, the variance components $\text{Var}[\alpha_i]$, $\text{Var}[\beta_i]$, $\text{Var}[\gamma_i]$, and their covariances. Those are the empirical bridge to $\mathcal{A}_g$ and $r_\text{env}$: first you estimate reaction-norm variation, then you reinterpret those estimates through the channel framework.

The quadratic reaction norm model for the MET phenotype is:

Quadratic reaction norm (Finlay–Wilkinson extended)
$$y_{ij} = \mu_j + \alpha_i + \beta_i \Delta u_j + \gamma_i \Delta u_j^2 + \varepsilon_{ij}$$

where $\mu_j$ absorbs the environment main effect, $\Delta u_j = u_j - u_0$ is the centred environmental state, and $(\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i)$ are random genotype-specific coefficients. At this stage, think of them as: level, slope, and curvature. Under genomic mixed models, their joint distribution is:

Joint distribution of reaction norm coefficients
$$\begin{pmatrix} \alpha_i \\ \beta_i \\ \gamma_i \end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\; \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2_\alpha & \sigma_{\alpha\beta} & \sigma_{\alpha\gamma} \\ \sigma_{\alpha\beta} & \sigma^2_\beta & \sigma_{\beta\gamma} \\ \sigma_{\alpha\gamma} & \sigma_{\beta\gamma} & \sigma^2_\gamma \end{pmatrix} \otimes \hat{G}\right)$$

Under the structural assumptions developed in the later apps, the scalar variance components can be identified with channel-framework objects as follows:

Identification dictionary
$$\sigma^2_\beta = V_{\text{lin,GEI}} = b_u^\top G b_u, \qquad \sigma^2_\gamma = V_{\text{quad,GEI}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$$ $$\sigma_{\alpha\beta} = b_0^\top G b_u, \qquad \sigma^2_\alpha = V_{\text{lin}}^{(0)} + V_{\text{quad}}^{(0)} = c_{00}$$

Fitting this model (in ASReml, the R packages asreml or breedR, or any genomic BLUP software with random regression capability) produces the empirical variance components needed for the channel diagnostics. The later apps show the identification step in detail, rather than asking you to accept the mapping on faith.

4

The bridge to the channel framework

Papers 1 and 2 of this series derived a diagnostic framework based on three objects: the fitness gradient $b$, the fitness Hessian $H$, and the genetic covariance matrix $G$. They showed that a population's additive predictability — captured by $\mathcal{A}_g$ — depends on how these objects interact.

The present paper asks: where do $b$, $H$, and $G$ come from in a real breeding program? The answer is a MET:

  • $G$ comes from genomic data. The additive genetic covariance matrix is estimated from SNP marker data as the genomic relationship matrix $\hat{G}$. This is standard in modern breeding.
  • $b$ and $H$ at the reference environment come from selection analysis. A Lande-Arnold regression of relative fitness on breeding values estimates the local linear selection gradient $b_0$ and the local quadratic terms collected in $H_0$.
  • $b_u$ and $H_u$ — the environmental sensitivities — are linked to the reaction norm variance components estimated in the MET. This is the new theoretical step. The variance of reaction-norm slopes, $\sigma^2_\beta$, is interpreted as the linear GEI quantity $b_u^\top G b_u$, and the variance of the curvature-like term is interpreted as $\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$, provided the structural assumptions of the framework hold.
The key insight of this series

Plant breeders have long estimated variance components for linear and nonlinear environmental response in MET analyses. What is new here is the theoretical reinterpretation: under the channel framework, those familiar variance components can be mapped onto the environmental sensitivity of a fitness landscape's gradient and curvature. This series makes that bridge explicit and shows what it implies for the reliability of genomic prediction under environmental change.

From an evolutionary biology perspective, the MET is a controlled version of natural selection across a heterogeneous landscape. The environmental gradient $u_j$ corresponds to the environmental state variable in Paper 2's transport laws. The reaction norm of a genotype is its phenotypic expression of the underlying fitness surface at each point along that gradient.

The channel framework makes a precise prediction: the rate of channel erosion due to environmental change is $r_\text{env} \propto \dot{u}$, where the proportionality constant involves exactly the ratio $\sigma^2_\beta / \sigma^2_\alpha$ (linear-to-total GEI fraction) and $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$ (covariance of mean performance and environmental sensitivity). Both are standard MET outputs.

In evolutionary terms: a population whose best-performing genotypes also happen to be the most environmentally responsive (positive $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$) will maintain a deeper additive channel as the environment shifts, because the fitness gradient and the direction of greatest heritable variance remain aligned. A population where the most responsive genotypes are average performers (negative $\sigma_{\alpha\beta}$) will see its additive channel erode faster — an Act III channel exit driven by gradient rotation.

The identification roadmap

The structural theorem at the heart of this series (derived in the companion paper, §3–4) states that, after choosing a scalar environmental coordinate $u$, the cross-environment genetic covariance can be written as a low-order polynomial in the centred environmental values:

Structural model — the key result (Theorem, companion paper §4)
$$C^{(G)}_{jk} = \underbrace{c_{00}}_{\sigma^2_\alpha} + \underbrace{c_{10}}_{\sigma_{\alpha\beta}}\!(\Delta u_j + \Delta u_k) + \underbrace{c_{11}}_{\sigma^2_\beta + \sigma^2_\gamma}\!\Delta u_j \Delta u_k$$

with exact identifications:

From MET coefficients to channel objects
$$c_{00} = V_{\text{lin}}^{(0)} + V_{\text{quad}}^{(0)}, \quad c_{10} = b_0^\top G b_u + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[H_0 G H_u G], \quad c_{11} = b_u^\top G b_u + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$$

These three scalars are identified by regressing the estimated covariance entries $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ on three basis functions of the environmental covariate, which requires at least three distinct environments with distinct $u$ values. The complete identification theorem and all derivations are in the companion paper, §3–5. Apps 3–5 in this series work through those steps with numerical examples.
