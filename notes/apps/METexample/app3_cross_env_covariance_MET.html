Cross-Environment
Genetic Covariance

The $E \times E$ genetic covariance matrix has a precise structural form under the channel model. Three numbers — $c_{00}$, $c_{10}$, $c_{11}$ — carry all the channel information hidden in it.

C(G)jk = c00 + c10(Δuj + Δuk) + c11 ΔujΔuk
1

What is $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ and how is it computed?

When a plant breeder runs a multi-environment trial, every pair of environments shares genetic signal. A line that out-yields the average in Queensland also tends to out-yield the average in New South Wales — to some degree. The cross-environment genetic covariance $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ measures exactly how much the genotypic performance in environment $j$ and the genotypic performance in environment $k$ move together across the lines in the trial.

Think of it as a genetic correlation, but expressed as a covariance rather than a scaled correlation: it mixes the magnitude of the genetic signal with its consistency across environments. High $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ for a pair of environments means genotype rankings are nearly identical between them — the same lines win in both. Low $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ means the rankings differ substantially — a crossover G×E.

Key point

The full $E \times E$ matrix $\mathbf{C}^{(G)}$ contains all the G×E information from the trial in one object. The structural model says this matrix is not arbitrary: under the channel model, it is a quadratic polynomial in the environmental covariates. That structure is what allows the identification of $V_{\text{lin,GEI}}$ and $V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$ from only three numbers.

The cross-environment genetic covariance has a direct biological reading. It is the covariance, across genotypes, of genotypic values in two environments:

  • If all genotypes respond identically to the environment (pure environmental main effect, no G×E), then $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ equals $\sigma^2_g$ for all pairs — a constant matrix. This is the "no G×E" baseline.
  • Linear G×E (Finlay–Wilkinson slopes) causes $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ to vary with the product $\Delta u_j \Delta u_k$. Environments far apart in the gradient have lower genetic covariance because responsive lines gain a large advantage in favourable environments while stable lines do not — splitting the rankings.
  • Nonlinear G×E (curvature) adds a further systematic pattern: environments at the extremes of the gradient have higher covariance with each other than with middle environments, because specialist genotypes (large $|\gamma_i|$) perform similarly at both extremes.

Each of these contributions is captured by one of the three coefficients $c_{00}$, $c_{10}$, $c_{11}$ in the structural model.

$C^{(G)}_{jk}$ is estimated from the MET by fitting the genomic mixed model with unstructured $E \times E$ genetic covariance:

Unstructured genomic mixed model
$$\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\tau} + Z_g\,\mathbf{g} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \quad \text{Var}(\mathbf{g}) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(G)} \otimes \hat{G}$$

The REML estimator of the $E \times E$ matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(G)}$ gives $\hat{C}^{(G)}_{jk} = \hat{\sigma}^{(G)}_{jk}$. In a factor analytic model with $K$ factors, the estimator is:

Factor analytic estimator
$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(G)} = \hat{\Lambda}\hat{\Lambda}^\top + \hat{\Psi}$$

where $\hat{\Lambda}$ is the $E \times K$ loading matrix and $\hat{\Psi}$ is a diagonal specific-variance matrix. The FA model is the dominant practical approach because it scales well to large $E$ and provides a low-rank approximation that is numerically stable. In ASReml-R the specification is fa(env, k); in sommer it is vsr(usr(fa(env,k)), line, Gvar=Ghat).

The structural model — deriving the three coefficients

The structural model is the central result of this app. It says that the cross-environment genetic covariance between environments $j$ and $k$ is a quadratic polynomial in the environmental covariates, with exactly three free parameters:

c00
Total genotypic variance at the reference environment $u_0$. Equal to $V_\text{lin}^{(0)} + V_\text{quad}^{(0)}$ — the sum of all heritable fitness variance at the reference point. This is what $\hat{\sigma}^2_g$ estimates in a single-environment analysis.
c10
Rate of change of the genetic covariance as one environment departs from the reference. Contains both the linear mixed term $b_0^\top G b_u$ and the Hessian mixed term $\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[H_0 G H_u G]$. Positive $c_{10}$ means the genetic signal strengthens as environments become more extreme.
c11
Total GEI variance — the variance of genotypic effects that depends on the environment. Equal to $V_{\text{lin,GEI}} + V_{\text{quad,GEI}} = b_u^\top G b_u + \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$. This is the quantity that App 10 separates into its linear and nonlinear components.
Why only three numbers?

Because the fitness surface is quadratic in $z$ and linear in $u$ (the assumptions of the channel model), and the covariance is a product of two such expansions averaged over a Gaussian distribution. The Gaussian kills all odd moments and the Isserlis theorem controls all even ones, leaving exactly the three-term structure $c_{00} + c_{10}(\cdot) + c_{11}(\cdot)$. An $E \times E$ matrix with $E(E+1)/2$ free entries collapses to just 3 numbers under this structure.

Each coefficient has a biological signature that a breeder can recognise in data:

  • Large $c_{00}$, small $c_{11}$: genotypes differ enormously in mean performance (high $\sigma^2_\alpha$) but respond similarly to the environment. G×E is negligible. The same genomic model works across all environments — prediction transfers perfectly.
  • $c_{11} \approx c_{00}$: as much genetic variance arises from G×E as from mean differences. Genotype rankings shift dramatically across environments. Single-environment GBLUP predictions transfer poorly.
  • $c_{10} > 0$: high-performing genotypes tend to be more responsive (positive $b_0^\top G b_u$). Genotypes that do well at the reference environment also gain more as conditions improve. This is the common pattern in high-input breeding programs where selection pressure under favourable conditions has linked mean performance to responsiveness.
  • $c_{10} < 0$: high performers are less responsive — a stability–performance tradeoff. The best genotypes at the reference are the most stable, not the most responsive. This sometimes occurs when breeding under stress has favoured constitutively high-performing lines.

The three-coefficient structure means that the cross-environment covariance $C^{(G)}_{jk}$ lies on a ruled surface in 3D space parametrised by $(\Delta u_j, \Delta u_k, C^{(G)}_{jk})$. The structural model is derived in the companion paper (§3). Starting from the quadratic genotypic value model and applying Gaussian closure and the Isserlis theorem, the result is:

Structural model (companion paper, Theorem §4)
$$C^{(G)}_{jk} = c_{00} + c_{10}(\Delta u_j + \Delta u_k) + c_{11}\,\Delta u_j\Delta u_k$$

with exact coefficient identifications:

Identification of structural coefficients
$$c_{00} = b_0^\top G b_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_0 G)^2] = V_\text{lin}^{(0)} + V_\text{quad}^{(0)}$$ $$c_{10} = b_0^\top G b_u + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[H_0 G H_u G]$$ $$c_{11} = b_u^\top G b_u + \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}[(H_u G)^2] = V_{\text{lin,GEI}} + V_{\text{quad,GEI}}$$

The OLS estimator uses the three basis functions $x^{(1)}_{jk} = 1$, $x^{(2)}_{jk} = \Delta u_j + \Delta u_k$, $x^{(3)}_{jk} = \Delta u_j\Delta u_k$ evaluated at all $\binom{E}{2} + E$ pairs $(j,k)$ with $j \leq k$:

OLS identification (requires $E \geq 3$)
$$\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{00}\\\hat{c}_{10}\\\hat{c}_{11}\end{pmatrix} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \operatorname{vech}(\hat{C}^{(G)})$$

where $\operatorname{vech}(\hat{C}^{(G)})$ stacks the lower triangle of the observed matrix and $X$ has columns $[x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}, x^{(3)}]$. Full rank of $X^\top X$ requires the environmental covariates to take at least 3 distinct values — the minimum MET design constraint.

The factor analytic model and its channel interpretation

Factor analytic (FA) models for MET data have been used in plant breeding for over two decades. They decompose the $E \times E$ genetic covariance matrix into a small number of latent factors. Breeders routinely fit FA(2) models — two-factor decompositions — to large METs.

The channel framework gives this familiar output a new interpretation: under the structural model, an FA(2) decomposition can often be read as a linear-plus-curvature split. The first factor tends to align with linear GEI (reaction norm slopes), while the second tends to align with quadratic GEI (reaction norm curvatures), provided the environmental gradient is well captured by the chosen covariate and the loading patterns follow the expected linear and U-shaped forms.

This means that a breeding program already fitting FA(2) models is very close to the quantities the channel diagnostic needs. When the FA loading patterns are consistent with the structural model, the ratio of the first factor variance to the total factor variance provides an empirical route to the GEI additivity index.

The biological interpretation of FA factors in the channel framework is sharper than the usual "latent mega-environment" story.

FA Factor 1
β
Linear GEI — reaction norm slopes. Each genotype's score on Factor 1 is proportional to $\beta_i = b_u^\top \Delta z_i$. Factor 1 loadings $\hat{\lambda}_{j1}$ are proportional to $\Delta u_j$ — they increase linearly with the environmental gradient.
≈
Factor 1 variance $\approx V_{\text{lin,GEI}} = b_u^\top G b_u$. Genotypes with high Factor 1 scores are environmentally responsive.
FA Factor 2
γ
Quadratic GEI — reaction norm curvatures. Each genotype's score on Factor 2 is proportional to $\gamma_i = \frac{1}{2}\Delta z_i^\top H_u \Delta z_i$. Factor 2 loadings $\hat{\lambda}_{j2}$ are proportional to $\Delta u_j^2$ — they follow a U-shape along the environmental gradient.
≈
Factor 2 variance $\approx V_{\text{quad,GEI}} = \frac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(H_u G)^2]$. Genotypes with high Factor 2 scores are environmental specialists.
Practical consequence

Inspect the FA(2) loading plot from your MET analysis. If Factor 1 loadings increase monotonically with the environmental gradient $u_j$ (linear pattern), and Factor 2 loadings form a U-shape (quadratic pattern), the structural model holds well. Deviations indicate higher-order terms or non-stationarity of the fitness surface across the environmental range.

Under the structural model, the $E \times E$ genetic covariance matrix admits a rank-2 approximation with structured loading patterns:

FA(2) decomposition under structural model
$$\Sigma^{(G)} = \Lambda\Lambda^\top + \Psi, \quad \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{11}&\lambda_{12}\\\vdots&\vdots\\\lambda_{E1}&\lambda_{E2}\end{pmatrix}$$

In the idealised structured-factor version of the model, the loading vectors take the polynomial form:

Structured loading vectors
$$\lambda_{j1} = \sqrt{V_{\text{lin,GEI}}}\;\frac{\Delta u_j}{\|\Delta\mathbf{u}\|}, \qquad \lambda_{j2} = \sqrt{V_{\text{quad,GEI}}}\;\frac{\Delta u_j^2 - \overline{\Delta u^2}}{\|\Delta\mathbf{u}^2 - \overline{\Delta u^2}\|}$$

The first loading vector is linear in $\Delta u_j$; the second is the centred quadratic $\Delta u_j^2$. These are the first two orthogonal polynomial contrasts of the environmental gradient — exactly what the legendre() specification in ASReml or the leg() function in sommer computes. The connection to the reaction norm model is therefore complete: the FA factors are the population-level loadings for the same polynomial basis that enters the individual-level reaction norm.

When those structured loading assumptions are adequate, the GEI additivity index from the FA output can be approximated by:

GEI additivity index from FA(2) output
$$\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}^2_{\lambda_1}}{\hat{\sigma}^2_{\lambda_1} + \hat{\sigma}^2_{\lambda_2}}$$

where $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\lambda_1}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\lambda_2}$ are the variances of the genotype scores on Factors 1 and 2 respectively — standard output from any FA model fit.

From $\hat{C}^{(G)}$ to channel coefficients — the full pipeline

Putting Sections 1–3 together, the complete workflow from raw MET data to the structural coefficients is:

  1. Fit the unstructured or FA model to the MET to obtain $\hat{C}^{(G)}_{jk}$ for all environment pairs.
  2. Compute the environmental basis functions $x^{(1)}_{jk}=1$, $x^{(2)}_{jk}=\Delta u_j+\Delta u_k$, $x^{(3)}_{jk}=\Delta u_j\Delta u_k$ for each pair.
  3. Run OLS of $\hat{C}^{(G)}_{jk}$ on the three basis functions to recover $\hat{c}_{00}$, $\hat{c}_{10}$, $\hat{c}_{11}$.
  4. Compute the additivity index at the reference environment: $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g = \hat{V}_{\text{lin}}^{(0)} / \hat{c}_{00}$ (requires $\hat{b}_0$ and $\hat{G}$ from App 7).
  5. Split $\hat{c}_{11}$ into $\hat{V}_{\text{lin,GEI}}$ and $\hat{V}_{\text{quad,GEI}}$ using the reaction norm variance components from App 8: $\hat{c}_{11} = \hat{\phi}_{\beta\beta} + \hat{\phi}_{\gamma\gamma}$. This is App 10's subject.

Two biological checks are useful before accepting the structural model fit:

  • Residual pattern: plot $\hat{C}^{(G)}_{jk} - (\hat{c}_{00} + \hat{c}_{10}(\cdot) + \hat{c}_{11}(\cdot))$ against $\Delta u_j \Delta u_k$. Systematic patterns indicate that the linear approximation in $u$ is insufficient — e.g. the fitness surface has a quadratic dependence on $u$ requiring a second-order extension.
  • Negative off-diagonal entries: if $\hat{C}^{(G)}_{jk} < 0$ for some pairs, the genotype rankings in those two environments are negatively correlated — a strong crossover interaction. The structural model can accommodate this only if $c_{11}$ is large enough relative to $c_{00}$. If $c_{11} > c_{00}$ and $c_{10}$ is large, the model predicts negative covariance at extreme environment pairs. This is biologically plausible in contrasting environments (e.g. tropical vs. temperate) and signals that the single environmental gradient $u$ is capturing most of the G×E structure.

The OLS normal equations in compact form. Let $\mathbf{c} = \operatorname{vech}(\hat{C}^{(G)})$ be the vector of estimated covariances (lower triangle, length $E(E+1)/2$), and let $X$ be the design matrix with columns $[1,\; \Delta u_j + \Delta u_k,\; \Delta u_j\Delta u_k]$:

OLS normal equations
$$X^\top X\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = X^\top \mathbf{c}, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{00}\\\hat{c}_{10}\\\hat{c}_{11}\end{pmatrix}$$

The $3 \times 3$ matrix $X^\top X$ depends only on the environmental design $\{u_j\}$. For a balanced design with $E$ environments equally spaced over $[-R, R]$, the design matrix has condition number of order $R^4$, so wide environmental ranges ($R$ large) give well-conditioned identification and small standard errors for $\hat{c}_{11}$. Narrow ranges ($R$ small) make $\hat{c}_{11}$ poorly identified — the quadratic basis function $\Delta u_j \Delta u_k$ has little variability.

The standard errors of $\hat{c}_{00}$, $\hat{c}_{10}$, $\hat{c}_{11}$ propagate from the estimation error in $\hat{C}^{(G)}_{jk}$. In turn, those depend on $n$ (lines) and the specific variances $\psi_j$. Bootstrap or delta method approximations are standard; App 10 gives the propagation formula for $\hat{\mathcal{A}}_g^{(\text{GEI})}$.

Legendre polynomial back-transformation

When $\hat{C}^{(G)}$ is estimated from an FA model using Legendre polynomial loadings (ASReml's fa() or rr()), the factor variances must be back-transformed from the orthogonal polynomial basis to the raw $\{1, \Delta u, \Delta u^2\}$ basis before plugging into the identification. The transformation matrix is $T = P^{-1}$ where $P$ is the Legendre-to-monomial change-of-basis matrix on $[-R, R]$. This is straightforward but easy to overlook — it is the source of the Legendre back-transformation note flagged in App 8.
