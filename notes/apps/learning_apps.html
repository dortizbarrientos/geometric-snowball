About these apps

A staged path through the mathematics

The paper can feel dense because it compresses several ideas into compact notation. These apps slow the argument down. Each step asks the same question in a slightly richer setting: when does a straight-line approximation describe selection well enough to be useful?

The sequence is deliberate. You begin with slope and curvature in one variable — high-school calculus. Then you add a population spread. Then you measure how much fitness variation the linear part captures. Then you watch that quantity change through time. Only after that do you explore the two-trait geometric cases.

No app requires anything beyond single-variable calculus and an intuition for normal distributions. The harder notation is introduced only after the idea it encodes is already visible in a picture.

The idea

Local linearity

Every smooth curve has a tangent line at every point. The tangent line is the best linear approximation to the curve at that point:

f(x) ≈ f(μ) + f'(μ)·(x − μ)

How good is this approximation? It depends on two things:

1. Curvature $f''(\mu)$. Strong curvature means the surface bends sharply — the tangent becomes unreliable quickly. Weak curvature means the surface is nearly flat over a wide region.

2. How far you are from $\mu$. The tangent is exact at $\mu$ itself. Errors grow as you move away.

Connection to the paper. In the paper, $\ell(z)$ is log-fitness, $\mu = \bar{z}$ is the population mean, $b = f'(\bar{z})$ is the fitness gradient, and $H = f''(\bar{z})$ is the Hessian. The tangent line is the additive prediction. This app shows when that prediction is trustworthy.
Try this. Set curvature to maximum, extra nonlinearity to zero. Move $\mu$ to the peak of the curve ($b \approx 0$). The tangent is flat — no directional selection. This is the Act II collapse.

The idea

The scaling asymmetry

The landscape has not changed between a narrow and a broad population. Only the spread changes. Yet the additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g$ falls as $\sigma$ rises. Why?

The linear variance $V_{\rm lin}$ involves one copy of $G = \sigma^2$:

V_lin = b² · G

The curvature variance $V_{\rm quad}$ involves two copies of $G$:

V_quad = ½ · H² · G²

So doubling $\sigma$ (quadrupling $G$) quadruples $V_{\rm lin}$ but sixteen-fold increases $V_{\rm quad}$. The curvature term grows faster, so $\mathcal{A}_g$ falls.

The important corollary. Under directional selection, $G$ compresses. Because $V_{\rm quad} \propto G^2$ falls faster than $V_{\rm lin} \propto G$, selection mechanically deepens the channel. A more selected, narrower population is always more locally additive — even without any change to the landscape.
Try this. Set $\sigma$ to maximum. Now hold $\sigma$ fixed and increase curvature. Notice that large $|H|$ can close the channel even when the population is narrow — but it has less impact than changing $\sigma$, because $\sigma$ appears twice.

The idea

Three acts

Act I (optimum moving, population lagging): $d$ is large, $V_{\rm lin} = (sd)^2 G$ is large, and $\mathcal{A}_g$ is high. The channel is open. Additive prediction works.

Act II (optimum reached, $d \to 0$): The gradient $b = sd$ collapses toward zero. $V_{\rm lin} \to 0$ while $V_{\rm quad}$ persists. $\mathcal{A}_g$ falls — not because the biology became complex, but because selection succeeded.

Act I again (optimum resumes moving): The lag rebuilds, $V_{\rm lin}$ recovers, $\mathcal{A}_g$ rises. Channel re-entry.

The counterintuitive result. A population near its optimum is less reliably additive than one far from it. The channel width is driven by the lag, not just by variance magnitude.
The rate diagnostic $r(t)$. Before $\mathcal{A}_g$ visibly falls, $r(t)$ goes negative. This is the early warning. A population can still be inside the channel while $r < 0$: prediction still works but is already being eroded.

The idea

Act III — misalignment without variance loss

This is the most important result for climate-change applications. The covariance cloud $\mathbf{G}$ has not changed. The curvature $\gamma$ has not changed. The only thing that changes is the direction of the fitness gradient.

Because $V_{\rm lin} = |\mathbf{b}|^2 (\lambda_1 \cos^2\theta + \lambda_2 \sin^2\theta)$ depends on alignment while $V_{\rm quad}$ does not, rotating $\mathbf{b}$ away from $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$ reduces $V_{\rm lin}$ and therefore $\mathcal{A}_g$ — without any loss of genetic variance and without any increase in landscape ruggedness.

How to read the plot. The curve shows $\mathcal{A}_g$ as a function of the angle $\theta$ between the gradient and $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$. It is highest when $\theta = 0$ (perfect alignment) and lowest when $\theta = 90°$ (gradient perpendicular to the major axis).
Key finding. A highly eccentric $\mathbf{G}$ (large $\lambda_1/\lambda_2$) is the most vulnerable to gradient rotation. The channel can exit at a small rotation angle when variance is concentrated in one direction. A rounder $\mathbf{G}$ is more robust to environmental rotation.
Try this. Set eccentricity ($\lambda_1/\lambda_2$) to maximum. Even a small rotation from $\theta = 0$ collapses $\mathcal{A}_g$ dramatically. Now set both eigenvalues equal (round $\mathbf{G}$). $\mathcal{A}_g$ is now flat — a round cloud is equally additive in all directions.

Directional geometry
Green: gradient $\mathbf{b}$. Amber: curvature-sensitive direction. Red: incoming variance direction $\mathbf{v}$.
The idea

New variance can help or hurt

When a wide cross or migration event introduces new genetic variance along direction $\mathbf{v}$, the change in $\mathcal{A}_g$ has two components:

δA_g ∝ (b·v)²/V_lin − v'HGHv / V_quad

The green benefit term is gradient alignment: how much the new variance points where selection wants to push. It grows when $\mathbf{v}$ is aligned with $\mathbf{b}$.

The amber cost term is curvature exposure: how much the new variance points into directions where the landscape bends. It grows when $\mathbf{v}$ is aligned with the direction of high curvature.

Practical implication. A wide cross that introduces variance aligned with the current fitness gradient deepens the channel and improves genomic prediction. A wide cross that introduces variance aligned with curvature directions loads $V_{\rm quad}$ and can harm prediction — even if it increases total genetic variance.
Try this. Align the gradient and the curvature-sensitive direction (set them to the same angle). Now any introgression direction is equally good or bad relative to both — no choice is optimal. Then separate them and find the optimal introgression direction for your current gradient.
