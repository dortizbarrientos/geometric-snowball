APP 01 / 05 · VARIANCE DECOMPOSITION

The Additivity Index
$\mathcal{A}_g$

"Why does a linear model predict evolutionary change in a population full of gene interactions?"

Prerequisite: none Key object: $\mathcal{A}_g = V_{\rm lin}/(V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad})$ Notebook: Section 2–3
01

The biological question

Genes interact. A single gene rarely acts alone — it cooperates with other genes in pathways, responds to environmental conditions, and produces effects that depend on genetic background. Given this complexity, why do additive models — models that treat every gene independently — predict short-term evolutionary change and breeding response so reliably?

Think of a fitness landscape as a hilly terrain. A population occupies a small region of that terrain — the region its current genetic variation can reach. If you zoom in on any small patch of a hilly surface, it looks nearly flat. Flat means linear. Linear means additive prediction works.

The key insight: it is not the global landscape that matters, but the local patch the population actually samples.

The fitness landscape is a curved surface $\ell(\mathbf{z})$ in trait space. The population occupies a cloud of breeding values centred at $\bar{\mathbf{z}}$, with spread described by the covariance matrix $\mathbf{G}$.

Any smooth surface, when restricted to a small neighbourhood, is well approximated by its tangent plane — the first-order (linear) term of a Taylor expansion. The question is how small the neighbourhood must be for that approximation to be adequate. The additivity index measures exactly that.

In the 1977 drought on Daphne Major, Geospiza fortis beak depth shifted nearly a millimetre in one generation. Heritability was high, the covariance cloud was elongated along the depth axis, and the selection gradient pointed along that same axis. The linear approximation was excellent — and Lande's equation predicted the response faithfully.

But as the population closed the gap to the new optimum, the directional signal collapsed. The landscape looked less and less like a tilted plane. Additive prediction began to fail. This transition from predictable to unpredictable is precisely what $\mathcal{A}_g$ tracks.

02

The variance decomposition

We begin by expanding the log-fitness surface to second order around the population mean $\bar{\mathbf{z}}$.

LOCAL LOG-FITNESS EXPANSION · Paper Eq. 1
$$\ell(\mathbf{z}) \approx \ell(\bar{\mathbf{z}}) + \underbrace{\mathbf{b}^\top \Delta\mathbf{z}}_{L\;=\;\text{linear part}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{H}\,\Delta\mathbf{z}}_{Q\;=\;\text{quadratic part}}$$
$\Delta\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z} - \bar{\mathbf{z}}$ is the deviation from the mean. $\mathbf{b} = \nabla\ell(\bar{\mathbf{z}})$ is the gradient. $\mathbf{H} = \nabla^2\ell(\bar{\mathbf{z}})$ is the Hessian (curvature).

Step 1 — Why do the variances add without a cross-term?

When the distribution of breeding values is symmetric (bell-shaped, centred at the mean), something beautiful happens: the linear part $L$ and the quadratic part $Q$ are uncorrelated. Their total variance is just the sum of their individual variances.

This is the same reason that the mean and variance of a normal distribution carry completely independent information — symmetry decouples them.

$L = \mathbf{b}^\top\Delta\mathbf{z}$ changes sign when you flip $\Delta\mathbf{z} \to -\Delta\mathbf{z}$ (it is an odd function — antisymmetric). $Q = \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\mathbf{z}^\top\mathbf{H}\Delta\mathbf{z}$ does not change sign (it is an even function — symmetric).

For a symmetric distribution, integrating odd × even over all deviations always gives zero. The cross-covariance $\text{Cov}(L, Q) = \mathbb{E}[LQ] = 0$. The two parts of the fitness landscape are geometrically orthogonal.

In a large, randomly mating population, the distribution of breeding values around the mean is approximately Gaussian. This Gaussian closure is the biological assumption that makes the whole framework work.

The assumption holds best when: many loci contribute small effects, selection is weak relative to recombination, and no single locus has a major effect. Under those conditions, the central limit theorem pushes the distribution toward Gaussian, and the cross-term vanishes.

Assume $\Delta\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{G})$. The cross-covariance requires: $$\mathbb{E}[LQ] = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k} b_i H_{jk}\,\mathbb{E}[\Delta z_i\,\Delta z_j\,\Delta z_k]$$ For a centred multivariate Gaussian, all odd central moments vanish: $\mathbb{E}[\Delta z_i\,\Delta z_j\,\Delta z_k] = 0$. Therefore $\mathbb{E}[LQ] = 0$, and since $\mathbb{E}[L] = 0$, we get $\text{Cov}(L,Q) = 0$.

Consequence: $\text{Var}[\delta\ell] = \text{Var}[L+Q] = \text{Var}[L] + \text{Var}[Q]$.

Step 2 — Computing $V_{\rm lin}$ and $V_{\rm quad}$

The gradient variance $V_{\rm lin}$ measures how much fitness variation is "slope-driven" — how much the population can gain by moving uphill along the gradient. A large $V_{\rm lin}$ means the gradient is strong and additive prediction will work well.

The curvature variance $V_{\rm quad}$ measures how much fitness variation is "curvature-driven" — how much the fitness surface bends across the population's spread. A large $V_{\rm quad}$ means the linear approximation misses important nonlinearity.

$V_{\rm lin} = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b}$ is the variance of a linear projection: how spread out the population is along the gradient direction. It depends on one copy of $\mathbf{G}$.

$V_{\rm quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{G})^2]$ captures how much the curvature of the landscape spreads fitness values across the covariance cloud. It depends on two copies of $\mathbf{G}$. This asymmetry — one copy versus two — is the most important structural fact in the whole framework.

$V_{\rm lin}$ is precisely the quantity that appears in Fisher's fundamental theorem: the directional component of heritable fitness variance per generation. It is what drives the mean phenotype uphill each generation under Gaussian closure.

$V_{\rm quad}$ is what the nonlinear selection gradient ($\boldsymbol\gamma$ matrix of Lande and Arnold) contributes to fitness variation. When $V_{\rm quad}$ is large relative to $V_{\rm lin}$, the $\boldsymbol\gamma$ matrix matters — and additive prediction loses accuracy.

From the quadratic form identity $\text{Var}[\mathbf{b}^\top\Delta\mathbf{z}] = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b}$ (no Gaussianity required): $$V_{\rm lin} = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b}$$ For the quadratic variance, apply Isserlis' theorem to the fourth moments of a centred Gaussian: $\mathbb{E}[\Delta z_i\Delta z_j\Delta z_k\Delta z_l] = G_{ij}G_{kl} + G_{ik}G_{jl} + G_{il}G_{jk}$. After index summation: $$V_{\rm quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{G})^2]$$ The factor $1/2$ comes from the $(1/2)$ in front of $Q$, and $\text{tr}[(\mathbf{HG})^2]$ collects all the cross-products of Hessian and covariance entries.

VARIANCE DECOMPOSITION · Paper Eq. 2
$$\text{Var}[\delta\ell] = \underbrace{V_{\rm lin}}_{\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b}} + \underbrace{V_{\rm quad}}_{\tfrac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{G})^2]}$$
One copy of $\mathbf{G}$ in $V_{\rm lin}$. Two copies in $V_{\rm quad}$. This asymmetry drives everything that follows.
🔑

Under uniform rescaling $\mathbf{G} \to s\mathbf{G}$ with fixed $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{H}$: $V_{\rm lin}(s) = s\,V_{\rm lin}$ and $V_{\rm quad}(s) = s^2\,V_{\rm quad}$.

Variance compression deepens a channel because $V_{\rm quad}$ falls faster than $V_{\rm lin}$. A narrower covariance cloud is always more locally additive.
03

The additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g$

Having split total log-fitness variance into gradient and curvature contributions, we define the additivity index as their ratio.

ADDITIVITY INDEX · Paper Eq. 3
$$\mathcal{A}_g = \frac{V_{\rm lin}}{V_{\rm lin} + V_{\rm quad}} = \frac{\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b} + \tfrac{1}{2}\text{tr}[(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{G})^2]}$$
$\mathcal{A}_g \in [0,1]$. High $\mathcal{A}_g$ means gradient dominates. Low $\mathcal{A}_g$ means curvature dominates.

$\mathcal{A}_g$ is the fraction of heritable fitness variance that a linear model captures. If $\mathcal{A}_g = 0.90$, a linear predictor explains 90% of local heritable fitness variance — and Lande's equation will work very well. If $\mathcal{A}_g = 0.20$, curvature dominates and additive prediction will be unreliable.

The channel threshold $\alpha$ (typically 0.70) separates populations inside a predictive regime ($\mathcal{A}_g > \alpha$) from those outside.

Geometrically, $\mathcal{A}_g$ measures how well the tangent plane at $\bar{\mathbf{z}}$ approximates the fitness surface across the population's covariance cloud. When the cloud is narrow relative to the curvature of the landscape, the tangent plane is a good approximation and $\mathcal{A}_g$ is close to 1. When the cloud is broad, the surface bends appreciably across it and $\mathcal{A}_g$ falls.

Crucially, $\mathcal{A}_g$ depends on all three objects jointly: the cloud $\mathbf{G}$, the gradient $\mathbf{b}$, and the curvature $\mathbf{H}$. None alone is sufficient.

$\mathcal{A}_g$ simultaneously captures the three biologically distinct ways that additive prediction can fail:

Route 1: the gradient $\mathbf{b}$ collapses (population nears optimum). $V_{\rm lin} \to 0$ even if $\mathbf{G}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are unchanged.

Route 2: the curvature $\mathbf{H}$ strengthens (population enters strongly curved region). $V_{\rm quad}$ rises.

Route 3: the covariance cloud $\mathbf{G}$ expands or rotates into directions of high curvature. $V_{\rm quad}$ rises faster than $V_{\rm lin}$.

$\mathcal{A}_g$ is defined as the proportion of total log-fitness variance explained by the linear term:

$$\mathcal{A}_g = \frac{V_{\rm lin}}{V_{\rm lin} + V_{\rm quad}}$$

It satisfies $\mathcal{A}_g \in (0,1)$ whenever $\mathbf{b} \neq \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{H} \neq \mathbf{0}$. Its behaviour under rescaling is immediate from the one-copy versus two-copy asymmetry: as $s \to 0$ (covariance collapses), $\mathcal{A}_g \to 1$; as $s \to \infty$, $\mathcal{A}_g \to 0$.

04

$\mathcal{A}_g$ is a local $R^2$

The additivity index has a clean statistical interpretation that connects it directly to the standard notion of predictive accuracy.

$\mathcal{A}_g$ equals the squared correlation between the linear predictor of fitness ($L = \mathbf{b}^\top\Delta\mathbf{z}$) and actual fitness ($\delta\ell = L + Q$). In other words, it is the $R^2$ of the best linear regression of log-fitness on breeding values — measured locally, at the current population position.

This is exactly the quantity a breeder reports when they assess genomic prediction accuracy: how well a linear genomic model explains fitness-related trait variation.

Since $\delta\ell = L + Q$ and $\text{Cov}(L,Q) = 0$ (under Gaussian closure), the regression of $\delta\ell$ on $L$ has: $$R^2 = \frac{\text{Var}[L]}{\text{Var}[\delta\ell]} = \frac{V_{\rm lin}}{V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad}} = \mathcal{A}_g$$ The uncorrelated structure means the linear part explains exactly the fraction $\mathcal{A}_g$ of total variance — no more, no less.

This interpretation closes the loop between the theoretical framework and empirical genomic prediction. When breeders report that a GBLUP model explains 75% of phenotypic variance for a trait under directional selection, that 75% is (roughly) an empirical estimate of $\mathcal{A}_g$ for that trait at that moment in the breeding programme.

As the population approaches a selection plateau and the directional gradient collapses, $R^2$ drops — not because the genomic model failed, but because the biology entered Act II.

The proof is a single line. Under Gaussian closure, $\text{Cov}(L,Q)=0$, so: $$\text{Corr}(L,\,\delta\ell)^2 = \frac{[\text{Cov}(L,L+Q)]^2}{\text{Var}[L]\cdot\text{Var}[L+Q]} = \frac{\text{Var}[L]^2}{\text{Var}[L]\cdot(\text{Var}[L]+\text{Var}[Q])} = \frac{V_{\rm lin}}{V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad}} = \mathcal{A}_g$$ The same uncorrelated structure also yields both classical results as special cases. Under weak selection, $\Delta\bar{\mathbf{z}} = \text{Cov}(\Delta\mathbf{z},\delta\ell)$. Since $\text{Cov}(\Delta\mathbf{z},Q) = \mathbf{0}$ (parity), only $L$ contributes: $$\Delta\bar{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{b} \quad \text{(Lande's equation)}$$ Premultiplying by $\mathbf{b}^\top$ gives $\Delta\bar\ell = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{G}\mathbf{b} = V_{\rm lin}$ (Fisher's theorem).

🔑

Fisher's variance argument, Lande's response equation, and Schluter's genetic lines of least resistance are all valid inside a locally additive channel.

They are not assumptions about global genetic architecture. They are local tangent-plane descriptions, valid precisely when $\mathcal{A}_g$ is high. The channel framework situates them rather than replacing them.
