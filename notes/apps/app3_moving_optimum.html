APP 03 / 05 · ONE-TRAIT MOVING-OPTIMUM MODEL

The Moving Optimum

"Why does a population near its fitness peak predict poorly — even though selection is still strong?"

Prerequisite: App 1 Key formula: $\mathcal{A}_g = d^2/(d^2+G/2)$ Notebook: Section 5
01

The paradox of success

Here is one of the most counterintuitive results in the framework: as a population succeeds at tracking a moving optimum and reduces its lag, additive prediction gets worse. Selection does not weaken — the population is still being strongly selected. But the channel closes.

Imagine a fitness peak moving slowly through trait space — like a climate target drifting year by year. When the population is far behind, there is a strong directional pull. The landscape looks like a tilted plane. Linear models work beautifully.

As the population catches up, the tilted plane becomes a bowl. The gradient weakens. Curvature dominates. The linear model no longer describes selection well — not because the biology became more complex, but because selection succeeded.

Near the optimum, the gradient $b = s \cdot d$ approaches zero as the lag $d \to 0$. So $V_{\rm lin} = b^2 G = s^2 d^2 G \to 0$. But $V_{\rm quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}s^2 G^2$ depends on $s$ and $G$, not $d$. The curvature term has no dependence on the lag. So the ratio $\mathcal{A}_g = V_{\rm lin}/(V_{\rm lin}+V_{\rm quad})$ collapses as $d \to 0$ — not because of any change in genetic architecture, but because the signal (gradient) vanishes while the noise (curvature) stays constant.

In crop breeding, this is a yield plateau. After generations of directional selection toward a target environment, the population approaches the local optimum. The directional signal collapses. Genomic prediction models trained on earlier, more directionally selected generations become unreliable. Breeders interpret this as a model failure, but it is actually a biological transition — Act II in the three-act channel framework.

02

The one-trait model

Consider a single trait $z$ under Gaussian stabilising selection toward a moving optimum $\theta(t)$:

FITNESS LANDSCAPE · Paper Eq. 5 (scalar)
$$\ell(z,t) = \ell_0 - \tfrac{1}{2}\,s\,(z - \theta)^2$$
$s > 0$ is the strength of stabilising selection (peak width$^{-2}$). $d = \theta - \bar{z}$ is the lag between optimum and population mean. At the current mean, $b = sd$ and $H = -s$.

The fitness landscape is a downward parabola — a single peak at $\theta$, with width controlled by $s$. As the optimum moves, the peak drags the population after it. The lag $d$ is the gap between where the optimum currently is and where the population mean currently sits.

At the current mean position, the gradient $b = sd$ measures how steeply fitness increases toward the optimum. The curvature $H = -s$ (negative because the peak curves downward) is fixed by $s$ alone.

At $\bar{z}$: the tangent line has slope $b = s \cdot d$ (directional pull). The curvature is $H = -s$ (stabilising). Both enter $\mathcal{A}_g$: $V_{\rm lin} = b^2 G = s^2 d^2 G$ and $V_{\rm quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}H^2 G^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}s^2 G^2$. Notice that $s^2$ cancels in the ratio — an important simplification.

The Gaussian fitness function is the standard model in quantitative genetics since Lande (1976). Its parameters have clear biological meaning: $s$ is estimated from selection gradients in natural populations (Kingsolver et al. 2001), and $d$ is estimated from the deviation of the population mean from the trait optimum. Both are measurable in long-term field studies.

Evaluating the variance components at the population mean $\bar{z}$ (where $\Delta z = z - \bar{z}$): $$b = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z}\bigg|_{\bar{z}} = s(\theta - \bar{z}) = sd$$ $$H = \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial z^2}\bigg|_{\bar{z}} = -s$$ Therefore: $V_{\rm lin} = (sd)^2 G = s^2 d^2 G$ and $V_{\rm quad} = \tfrac{1}{2}s^2 G^2$.

03

The closed-form result

Substituting into the definition of $\mathcal{A}_g$ gives a beautifully simple result:

ADDITIVITY INDEX IN MOVING-OPTIMUM MODEL · Paper Eq. 6
$$\mathcal{A}_g = \frac{d^2}{d^2 + G/2}$$
The selection strength $s$ cancels. $\mathcal{A}_g$ depends only on the lag $d$ and the genetic variance $G$. This is one of the most elegant results in the framework.

The formula says: $\mathcal{A}_g$ rises with lag $d$ (the further the population is from the optimum, the better additive prediction works) and falls with genetic variance $G$ (the broader the cloud, the more curvature is sampled). Large $d$ and small $G$ together mean an excellent channel.

$\mathcal{A}_g \to 1$ as $d \to \infty$ or $G \to 0$. $\mathcal{A}_g \to 0$ as $d \to 0$ or $G \to \infty$. The halfway point $\mathcal{A}_g = 0.5$ occurs at $d^2 = G/2$, i.e., when the lag equals $\sqrt{G/2}$ — roughly the standard deviation of the covariance cloud.

The cancellation of $s$ is biologically profound: the predictability of evolution under a moving optimum does not depend on how strong the selection is. A weak stabilising gradient ($s$ small) and a strong one ($s$ large) produce the same $\mathcal{A}_g$, given the same $d$ and $G$. What matters is the geometry — lag versus spread.

From the variance components: $\mathcal{A}_g = s^2 d^2 G\, /\, (s^2 d^2 G + \tfrac{1}{2}s^2 G^2)$. Factor out $s^2 G$:

$$\mathcal{A}_g = \frac{d^2}{d^2 + G/2}$$

Mathematica confirms this in one line: FullSimplify[Vlin1D/(Vlin1D + Vquad1D)] returns the formula above, with $s$ absent from the result.

🔑

Selection strength $s$ cancels from $\mathcal{A}_g$. Predictability is a geometric property of lag and spread — not selection intensity.

Two populations with very different selection pressures but the same $d/\sqrt{G}$ ratio have identical channel depth. This geometric view separates the question "how strong is selection?" from "how predictable is the response?"
04

The optimum paradox and $r(t)$

The one-trait rate diagnostic has a full mechanistic decomposition. Each term is biologically interpretable:

RATE DIAGNOSTIC IN MOVING-OPTIMUM MODEL · Paper Eq. 7
$$r = \underbrace{\frac{2\dot\theta}{d}}_{\text{optimum motion}} - \underbrace{sG}_{\text{variance load}} + \underbrace{\lambda_{\rm rec}\!\left(1-\frac{G_*}{G}\right)}_{\text{recombination}} - \underbrace{\frac{m}{G}}_{\text{mutation}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2N_e}}_{\text{drift}}$$
Each term is signed: faster optimum motion deepens the channel; wider variance erodes it; drift always helps (narrows the cloud); mutation always hurts.

The $2\dot\theta/d$ term explains the paradox: when the optimum is stationary ($\dot\theta = 0$) and the population is close to it (small $d$), this term is zero. But the $-sG$ term remains, eroding the channel. The closer the population gets to the optimum, the faster $r$ falls toward zero and below — the channel is collapsing even though the population is succeeding.

Drift term $+1/2N_e$: always positive. Small populations are more locally additive because their narrower clouds sample less curvature. This is a geometric consequence of the one-vs-two copy asymmetry — not a biological simplification of small populations.

Mutation term $-m/G$: always negative. New mutations enter isotropically, increasing $G$ in all directions equally, which raises $V_{\rm quad}$ faster than $V_{\rm lin}$.

Optimum motion $+2\dot\theta/d$: this is why climate change can paradoxically open a channel — by increasing the lag and regenerating directional signal, even as it imposes environmental stress.

To derive $r$ in 1D, differentiate $\mathcal{L} = \ln(V_{\rm lin}) - \ln(V_{\rm quad})$ and substitute $\dot b = -s\dot d = -s(\dot\theta - G b)$ (from the Lande equation $\dot{\bar{z}} = Gb$ and the chain rule on $d = \theta - \bar{z}$): $$r = \frac{2\dot b}{b} - \frac{\dot G}{G} = \frac{2(\dot\theta - Gb)s}{sd} - \frac{\dot G}{G}$$ Substituting the covariance equation for $\dot G$ and simplifying gives all five terms. See SI Appendix Section S6.
