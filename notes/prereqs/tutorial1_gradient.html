What is a Gradient?

The direction that fitness pushes a population

Start here — no maths yet

A hill is a gradient you can see

Imagine you place a ball on a hillside. It rolls downhill. You didn't push it — the shape of the hill did. That shape has a direction (which way is steepest) and a magnitude (how steep it is). Together, those make a gradient.

In evolutionary biology, fitness is the hill. Breeding values — the heritable part of a trait — are the terrain. The fitness gradient $b$ tells us: in which direction, and how strongly, does selection currently push the population mean?

Biological reading: When $b > 0$, bigger values of the trait increase fitness. When $b < 0$, smaller values win. When $b = 0$, the population is at the top of the hill — a fitness optimum.
The maths — gently

Rise over run, but at a point

You probably know the slope of a line: $\text{rise}/\text{run}$. The gradient extends this idea to curved functions. It answers: how fast is fitness changing right here, right now?

Formally, at the population mean $\bar{z}$:

b = dℓ/dz |z̄

Read as: "the rate of change of log-fitness with respect to trait value, evaluated at the population mean." The vertical bar with $\bar{z}$ means we measure the slope at that specific point, not anywhere else.

Why log-fitness? We use log-fitness because fitness is multiplicative (a doubling is the same proportional change whether starting from 1 or 1000). Logarithms turn multiplication into addition, making the maths linear and tractable.

Slope changes along the curve

Look at the interactive hill on the right. As you drag the red dot along the fitness surface, the green tangent line shows the local gradient. Notice:

On the left slope, the tangent tilts up — positive gradient. Selection pushes the trait rightward.
At the peak, the tangent is flat — zero gradient. No net directional selection.
On the right slope, the tangent tilts down — negative gradient. Selection now pushes leftward.
In the paper

What $b$ does in the framework

The gradient $b$ appears everywhere in the paper because it is the engine of directional evolution. In Lande's equation:

Δz̄ = G · b

The population mean shifts each generation in proportion to $b$. More gradient → faster evolution. Zero gradient → stasis.

The additivity index $\mathcal{A}_g$ depends on $b$ through $V_{\rm lin} = b^\top \mathbf{G}\, b$. A large gradient means $V_{\rm lin}$ dominates the variance budget, the additive channel is open, and Lande's equation is reliable.

The key result (Act II): When a population reaches its fitness optimum, $b \to 0$ and $V_{\rm lin} \to 0$. The additive channel closes not because the genetics changed, but because the gradient collapsed.

Gradient vs curvature — what's the difference?

The gradient $b$ is the slope — the tilt of the tangent line. It drives the direction of evolution.

The curvature $H$ is the bend — how fast the slope itself changes. It drives stabilising or disruptive selection.

The paper's variance decomposition separates the heritable fitness variance into these two effects. High gradient → open channel. High curvature → closed channel.
