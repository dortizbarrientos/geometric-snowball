Variance, Local Thinking & Eigenvalues

The three remaining building blocks of the framework

Tutorial 03 — Core concept

Spread and shared spread

Variance answers: how spread out is a population? It is the average squared distance of each individual from the mean.

Var[z] = E[(z − z̄)²]

A high-variance population spans a wide range of trait values — lots of raw material for selection. A low-variance population is tightly clustered around its mean.

Covariance answers: do two traits tend to move together?

Cov[z₁, z₂] = E[(z₁ − z̄₁)(z₂ − z̄₂)]

Positive covariance: when one trait is above average, the other tends to be too. Negative: when one is high, the other tends to be low. Zero: no relationship.

The G matrix is just a table of variances and covariances. The diagonal entries are genetic variances of each trait. The off-diagonal entries are genetic covariances between pairs of traits.
Why variance appears in V_lin

One copy vs two copies of G

The paper's key structural fact is that $V_{\rm lin}$ involves one copy of $\mathbf{G}$ while $V_{\rm quad}$ involves two copies:

V_lin = b² · Var[Δz] = b²G
V_quad = ½H² · Var[Δz²] = ½H²G²

The second equation uses the fact that for a Gaussian variable $\Delta z$, the variance of $\Delta z^2$ equals $2G^2$. This is why $V_{\rm quad}$ has two powers of $G$.

The consequence: If you halve $G$ (selection compresses variance), $V_{\rm lin}$ halves but $V_{\rm quad}$ quarters. The curvature load shrinks faster. Narrow clouds are always more locally additive.

Tutorial 04 — Core concept

Every smooth curve is locally a straight line

Here is one of the most powerful ideas in mathematics: any smooth function, zoomed in close enough, looks like a straight line. Zoom in more and it looks like a parabola. Zoom in even more and you get higher-order terms.

This is Taylor's theorem. It says we can always approximate a smooth function near a point using a polynomial:

f(x) ≈ f(x₀) + f'(x₀)·(x−x₀) + ½f''(x₀)·(x−x₀)² + ...

In the paper, the function is log-fitness $\ell(z)$, the point is the population mean $\bar{z}$, the first derivative is the gradient $b$, and the second derivative is the Hessian $H$:

ℓ(z) ≈ ℓ(z̄) + b·Δz + ½H·Δz²
The local approximation works because the population's covariance cloud $G$ defines how far $\Delta z$ typically reaches. If that range is small relative to the curvature scale, the linear term dominates and additive prediction works.
Why two terms, not one?

The gradient alone isn't enough

The first term ($b \cdot \Delta z$) captures directional selection: the slope of the fitness surface at the mean. It drives mean evolution.

The second term ($\tfrac{1}{2}H \cdot \Delta z^2$) captures curvature: stabilising selection (bending toward a peak, $H < 0$) or disruptive selection (bending away, $H > 0$).

The key is that the variance of the first term is $b^2 G$ (linear in $G$) while the variance of the second term is $\tfrac{1}{2}H^2 G^2$ (quadratic in $G$). This asymmetry is everything.

Geometric picture: The first term is the tangent line. The second term is the gap between the tangent and the true curve. When the cloud is narrow, you barely see the gap. When it is wide, the gap dominates.

Tutorial 05 — Core concept

A matrix has natural directions

Every symmetric matrix — including $\mathbf{G}$ — has special directions called eigenvectors. When you apply the matrix to one of these directions, the vector doesn't rotate; it only stretches or shrinks.

The amount of stretching is the eigenvalue. Formally:

G · v = λ · v

Here $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector and $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue. The matrix $\mathbf{G}$ just multiplies $\mathbf{v}$ by $\lambda$ — no rotation, only scaling.

Biological reading: The eigenvectors of $\mathbf{G}$ are the principal axes of genetic variation — the directions in trait space along which breeding values are most and least spread. The eigenvalues are the amounts of spread along each axis.
g_max: the direction of most variation

The leading eigenvector

The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is called $\mathbf{g}_{\max} = e_1(\mathbf{G})$. It points in the direction of greatest genetic variance — the genetic line of least resistance.

Why "least resistance"? Because selection on a trait combination aligned with $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$ finds the most genetic variation to act on — evolution proceeds fastest in that direction.

Conversely, selecting for a trait combination perpendicular to $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$ finds very little genetic variance — evolution is slow or constrained.

Schluter (1996): Adaptive radiations tend to follow $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$ because it is the direction of maximum genetic evolvability. In the interactive panel, drag the selection gradient arrow and watch how the evolutionary response (blue arrow) is biased toward $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$.

Connection to the paper

The misalignment angle $\theta$ between the fitness gradient $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$ determines how much of the gradient-aligned variance $V_{\rm lin}$ the population can generate:

V_lin(θ) = |b|² · (λ₁cos²θ + λ₂sin²θ)

When $\mathbf{b}$ is aligned with $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$ ($\theta = 0$), $V_{\rm lin}$ is maximised and the channel is wide open. As the gradient rotates away, $V_{\rm lin}$ falls — this is Act III: channel exit from gradient misalignment.
