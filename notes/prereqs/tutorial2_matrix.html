What is the G Matrix?

Genetic variation is a shape, not a single number

The big idea

One trait: a variance. Two traits: an ellipse.

For a single trait, you summarise genetic variation with one number: the additive genetic variance $G$ (or sometimes written $V_A$). Bigger $G$ means the population spans a wider range of breeding values — more raw material for selection.

But most traits do not evolve in isolation. Beak depth and beak width. Flowering time and drought tolerance. Leaf area and stem thickness. When two traits share genetic pathways, changing one tends to change the other. That genetic correlation between traits cannot be captured by a single number.

The solution is the additive genetic covariance matrix G — a square table of numbers that captures both the variance of each trait and the covariance between pairs of traits. It encodes the shape of genetic variation in multi-trait space.

Geometric reading: $\mathbf{G}$ is the shape of the ellipse you would draw around the cloud of breeding values. A round ellipse → traits vary independently. An elongated ellipse → traits vary together (are genetically correlated).
Reading the matrix

What each number means

For two traits ($z_1$ and $z_2$), $\mathbf{G}$ has four entries:

[
G₁₁G₁₂ G₁₂G₂₂
]

Diagonal entries ($G_{11}$, $G_{22}$): the additive genetic variance of each trait on its own. Always positive. Larger = more heritable spread in that trait.

Off-diagonal entries ($G_{12}$): the additive genetic covariance between traits. Can be positive (traits move together genetically) or negative (traits oppose each other).

Genetic correlation: The covariance normalised: $r_A = G_{12}/\sqrt{G_{11}\,G_{22}}$. Ranges from −1 to +1. Zero = genetically independent. +1 = always evolve together. −1 = selection on one perfectly opposes the other.

Why G matters for evolution

G filters and biases the gradient

In Lande's equation, the evolutionary response is not just proportional to the gradient — it is filtered through G:

Δz̄ = G · b

This matrix multiplication has a crucial consequence: evolution does not follow the steepest gradient. It follows the path of greatest genetic variation. The eigenvectors of $\mathbf{G}$ (its principal axes) tell you which directions are genetically accessible.

Schluter's insight: Adaptive radiation tends to follow the major axis of $\mathbf{G}$ — the direction of most genetic variance — because that path offers the least evolutionary resistance. This is the genetic line of least resistance, $\mathbf{g}_{\max} = e_1(\mathbf{G})$.

In the interactive ellipse, the dashed line shows $\mathbf{g}_{\max}$. Notice how it always points along the longest axis of the ellipse — the direction where breeding values spread most.

Three canonical G shapes

Left: $G_{12}=0$. Traits vary independently — a circle or axis-aligned ellipse. No genetic constraint between traits.

Centre: $G_{12}>0$. Positive correlation — one trait tends to come with the other genetically. Common when traits share developmental pathways.

Right: $G_{12}<0$. Negative correlation — genetic antagonism. Selecting for more of trait 1 tends to reduce trait 2.
